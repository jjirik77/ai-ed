    Week 7 · Module 7

    Assessment, Grading, Feedback, and Course Policy

    Driving questionHow can assessment produce valid evidence of learning when students and instructors have access to AI?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Assessment stress testArtifact: Assessment + policy

    Overview

    Validity first, then permission

    An assessment is valid when it produces trustworthy evidence of the outcome it claims to measure. AI does not change that definition. It changes which tasks still produce that evidence when students work unsupervised. In the Digital Education Council's 2025 faculty survey, 54% of faculty said current student evaluation methods are no longer adequate.

    This module works in a fixed order. Start with the outcome. Choose how much AI use fits that outcome. Redesign the task so the student's reasoning is visible. Then write directions students can follow and a policy that explains why. Grading comes last, with a firm rule: AI can help you draft feedback, but grades and misconduct decisions stay with you.

    Predict before you read

    You label a take-home essay "No AI allowed." What does that label actually control? What would you need to add for the label to be enforceable?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    On its own, the label controls very little. It states an expectation, which matters, but an unsupervised task cannot prevent AI use and detectors cannot reliably prove it. The authors of the AI Assessment Scale make the same point: the levels are a way to communicate expectations, not a security mechanism. To make "No AI" meaningful you need supervision, such as an in-class component or an oral check, or you need to choose a level you can stand behind and redesign the task so the evidence of learning survives.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Align the level of permitted AI use with the learning outcome of each assessment. Evaluate
    • Distinguish formative AI assistance from consequential grading decisions. Analyze
    • Redesign an assignment so process, reasoning, and judgment are visible. Create
    • Draft clear task-level AI directions and a course-level AI policy with a rationale. Create
    • Respond to suspected misuse fairly, without treating a detector score as proof. Evaluate

    Core ideas

    7.1

    Match the AI level to the outcome

    The AI Assessment Scale, revised by Perkins, Roe, and Furze in 2024, gives you and your students a shared vocabulary. Each level describes how AI may be used. No level is best in general. The right level is the one that still lets the task show the outcome.

    Two cautions from the scale's authors matter in practice. First, levels communicate expectations; they do not enforce them. A Level 1 or Level 2 label on an unsupervised take-home task cannot stop AI use. Second, the level should follow from the outcome. If the outcome is "solve equations without aids," Level 1 under supervision fits. If the outcome is "produce a professional marketing plan," banning the tools professionals use may lower authenticity without improving validity.

    In practice

    CTEA welding program keeps written safety-procedure quizzes at Level 1 in class, and allows Level 3 for a project report where students use AI to organize their notes but must include their own photos, measurements, and inspection results.

    7.2

    Redesign so the reasoning shows

    A redesign should measure the outcome better, not just make AI inconvenient. Eight moves do most of the work. Each one makes process or judgment visible, and each one has a cost.

    MoveWhat it showsCost and equity watch
    Staged submissionsThinking develops over time; feedback lands mid-taskMore deadlines to manage
    Process notes or annotated draftsChoices the student made and whyModel what good notes look like
    Local or personal contextApplication to a specific setting AI cannot knowNever require private personal disclosure; offer a local-community option
    Oral explanationThe student can explain and extend their workTime intensive; offer accommodations and a written alternative
    In-class checkpointA supervised sample of the skillPlan for absences and testing accommodations
    Source verificationStudents checked what they citeTeach lateral reading first (Module 4)
    AI interaction recordHow AI was used and what the student did with itOnly meaningful when AI is permitted; keep it short
    Reflective defenseJudgment about strengths, limits, and choicesShort and specific beats long and generic

    Two common moves look strong but do little. Requiring handwriting adds barriers for some students with disabilities without showing more reasoning. Running every submission through a detector adds workload and risk of false accusations without measuring the outcome. The interactive in 7.3 lets you test both.

      Most participants find that two or three well-chosen moves reach a valid design. Adding every move raises workload faster than validity. That trade-off is the point: choose the moves that show the specific thinking the outcome names.
      

      7.4
      AI in grading and feedback
      

      The default for AI in grading is formative support, not autonomous judgment. AI can help you draft comments faster and more consistently. It cannot be responsible for a grade. If you use AI for feedback, these practices apply:
      

      • You keep final grading authority and review every AI-assisted comment or recommendation before a student sees it.
        • 

      • You use only an institution-approved tool, never a personal consumer account, and remove identifiers where possible.
        • 

      • You tell students that AI helps draft feedback and how you review it.
        • 

      • You pilot the workflow on a few submissions and calibrate it against your own judgment before scaling.
        • 

      • You watch for inconsistency and bias, especially toward multilingual writers and nonstandard dialects.
        • 

      • Students have a clear path to ask for human review of any feedback.
      

      The example shows why review is not optional. The student paragraph is fictional.
      

      Our results show that plants given more light grew taller. Plants in the 12-hour group grew an average of 8.2 cm, compared with 5.1 cm in the 6-hour group. This proves that light causes growth in all plants.
      

      Draft feedback: Strong paragraph! You clearly report your data and correctly conclude that light causes growth in all plants. The average difference of 3.5 cm is statistically significant. Score: 9/10.
      

      Show the errors
      Four problems. The difference is 8.2 minus 5.1, which is 3.1 cm, not 3.5. Nothing in the paragraph supports "statistically significant," since no test is reported. The feedback praises the overgeneralization ("proves," "all plants") that the student most needs to fix. And it assigns a score, which is your decision, not the tool's. Useful feedback would name the clear data reporting, then ask the student what a single experiment with one species can and cannot show.
      

      Responsible use
      Never paste identifiable student work into a non-approved tool. Grades and misconduct decisions must be made, and be explainable, by a person.
      

      In practice
      HealthA nursing instructor uses an approved tool to draft rubric-aligned comments on de-identified care plans. The instructor edits each comment, adds one personal note, and enters scores herself. Students know the process and can request a meeting about any comment.
      

      7.5
      Write the policy and the task-level directions
      

      Students need two layers of guidance. The course policy explains your general expectations and the reasons behind them. Task-level directions on each assignment state the AI level, what is allowed, and how to disclose. When the two disagree, students should follow the task-level directions, and the policy should say so.
      

      Sample course AI policy
      

      AI in this course

This course builds your ability to [course-level outcome]. AI can support that learning when you use it on purpose and stay in charge of your thinking. It can also replace the practice you need. These expectations explain the difference.

1. Each assignment states an AI-use level, from Level 1 (No AI) to Level 5 (AI Exploration), and what that level allows. The assignment directions take priority over this general policy. If an assignment does not say, ask before using AI.
2. Use only [approved tool(s)] for coursework. Never enter other students' work, personal information, or [protected data] into an AI tool.
3. When you use AI, disclose it with the AI-use statement on the assignment. Honest disclosure is expected and is not penalized.
4. You are responsible for everything you submit, including errors that AI introduced. Check facts, sources, and calculations.
5. If I have questions about how work was produced, I will talk with you first and look at your process evidence. I do not treat AI-detector results as proof.
6. If you cannot or choose not to use AI, you will always have an option that meets the same outcome without it.
7. I may use [approved tool] to help draft feedback on your work. I read and edit every comment, and I alone assign grades. You can ask me to review any comment.

Questions: [office hours or contact]. Concerns about a decision follow [institutional appeal process].
      

      Task-level AI-use statement for students
      

      AI-use statement (attach to your submission)

Level for this assignment: [for example, Level 2, AI-Assisted Planning]
I used AI:  [ ] No   [ ] Yes
If yes, tool used: ______
What I used it for: ______
What I kept, changed, or rejected, and why: ______
I checked:  [ ] facts   [ ] sources   [ ] calculations
I can explain every part of this work in my own words.
      

      You can use AI to help draft your own policy, as long as you make the decisions first:
      

      Help me draft a course AI policy for [course name and level]. My decisions: the course outcomes are [outcomes]; the approved tool is [tool]; my default AI level is [level] with exceptions on specific assignments; I use AI to help draft feedback but assign all grades myself. Write it in plain language at about a 10th-grade reading level, under 300 words, with a short reason for each expectation. Do not add rules I did not list; mark anything you think is missing as a question for me.
      

      In practice
      Student supportA first-year seminar instructor reads the policy aloud in week one and asks students to rewrite rule 5 in their own words. The discussion surfaces fears about false accusations, which the instructor answers directly.
      

      7.6
      Respond to suspected misuse fairly
      

      AI detectors estimate whether text resembles model output. They do not establish who wrote it. In a 2023 study in Patterns, Liang and colleagues ran seven detectors on 91 TOEFL essays written by non-native English writers. The detectors labeled more than half of them as AI-generated, with an average false-positive rate of 61.3%. The same study found that simple prompting let AI-written text evade the detectors. A detector score can prompt a closer look. It is never sufficient evidence on its own.
      

      When you suspect misuse, follow a fair sequence:
      

      1. Check the task first. Were the AI directions for this assignment clear and specific? Ambiguity is a design problem, not a student offense.
        2. 

      2. Look at process evidence. Drafts, version history, notes, checkpoints, and the AI-use statement.
        3. 

      3. Talk with the student. Ask them to walk you through their work and choices. Keep the tone curious, not accusatory.
        4. 

      4. Decide proportionately. A misunderstanding of directions is a teaching moment. If concerns remain, follow your institution's integrity process.
        5. 

      5. Document facts, not impressions. Record what you saw and what the student said.
        6. 

      6. Confirm the appeal path. Make sure the student knows how to ask for review.

      Judgment check · unscored practice
      Test your judgment
      Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.
      Q1.An AI detector flags a multilingual student's essay as "92% likely AI-generated." The student has submitted solid work all term. What should you do next?
      Question 1 options
      Best answer: B. Detectors have documented false positives, especially for non-native English writers. Process evidence and a conversation give you real information, and the institutional process protects both of you.
      • A. A detector score is not proof, and reporting on that basis risks a false accusation.
      • C. Penalizing first reverses the burden of proof and is unfair if the flag is wrong.
      • D. The score alone is not grounds for action, but ignoring it entirely skips a fair look at the process. If other evidence raises real concerns, you still follow the process.
      Q2.The outcome is "solve linear equations fluently without aids," measured by a short readiness quiz. Which level and setting fit best?
      Question 2 options
      Best answer: C. The outcome is unaided skill, so No AI fits, and supervision is what makes that level meaningful.
      • A. AI help on the task would measure assisted performance, not the unaided skill named in the outcome.
      • B. A pledge states an expectation but cannot enforce Level 1 on an unsupervised task.
      • D. Exploration suits advanced, open problems, not a fluency check.
      Q3.The outcome is "evaluate sources for a research question." Which revision measures the outcome rather than just making AI inconvenient?
      Question 3 options
      Best answer: A. The log and conversation make the evaluation itself visible. They show the judgment the outcome names.
      • B. Handwriting adds barriers and does not show any more evaluation.
      • C. A short deadline adds pressure, not evidence, and disadvantages students with jobs or caregiving duties.
      • D. Ranking a supplied packet skips finding sources, which is part of evaluating them for a research question.
      Q4.You want to use an approved AI tool to draft formative comments on 80 lab reports. Which practice fits responsible use?
      Question 4 options
      Best answer: B. This keeps grading authority human, protects student data, and is transparent. Reviewing every comment catches errors like those in the 7.4 example.
      • A. Autonomous scoring with a sample scan removes the instructor accountability that grading requires.
      • C. Personal accounts are not approved for student records, however carefully you review.
      • D. Uneven use creates inconsistent treatment and does not address review or disclosure.
      Q5.Your syllabus says "AI use is prohibited unless permitted." Students keep asking what that means for each assignment. What is the best fix?
      Question 5 options
      Best answer: C. Students need guidance at the point of the task. Task-level directions with a reason turn a vague rule into something they can follow.
      • A. Product lists go out of date quickly and still do not say what is allowed on a given task.
      • B. Removing guidance increases confusion and inconsistency.
      • D. An open door helps, but it puts the burden on students and does not scale.
