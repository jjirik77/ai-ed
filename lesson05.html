    Week 5 · Module 5

    Instructional Materials, Accessibility, and Copyright

    Driving questionHow can AI speed up preparation without weakening alignment, accuracy, accessibility, or your authorship?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Improve, don't generateArtifact: Accessible material + QA record

    Overview

    Improve, do not merely generate

    AI can produce a handout, a case, or a set of practice items in seconds. The draft usually looks finished. It rarely is. It may drift from your outcome, test recall when you need analysis, bury the key idea in extra text, skip alt text and headings, and fill every example with the same kind of person.

    This module starts from what you want students to learn and works backward to the material. AI enters at the drafting step, and you own the steps around it: alignment, accuracy, accessibility, representation, and disclosure. The finished product should show your judgment, not just your prompt. That matters for students, and it also matters for authorship.

    Predict before you read

    You generate a one-page handout with AI, fix two typos, and post it. Under current U.S. Copyright Office guidance, who is the author of the handout's expressive content? What would change that?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    Under the Office's January 2025 report, prompts alone do not make you the author of AI-generated output, and fixing typos adds little. What can be protected is your own contribution: material you wrote, and your creative selection, arrangement, or modification of AI output. Substantive revision is good teaching practice, and it is also what makes the work yours. For any specific case, ask your institution's copyright or library contact.

    Study the material

    Core ideas

    5.1

    Start from the outcome, not the tool

    Before you prompt, write down the outcome the material serves and the evidence that will show students reached it. Then the prompt can name both, and your check has something to measure against. If you cannot name the outcome, the material is not ready to be drafted by anyone, human or AI.

    In practice

    HealthA respiratory therapy instructor's outcome is "interpret an arterial blood gas and identify the primary disorder." An AI-drafted handout lists normal ranges. That supports the outcome but does not practice it. The instructor keeps the ranges and adds three short cases students interpret.

    5.2

    Improve the draft: alignment, demand, and load

    Treat every AI draft as a first draft from a capable assistant who has never met your students. Check three things before you check anything else.

    CheckQuestionCommon AI pattern
    AlignmentDoes each part serve the named outcome?Extra sections on related topics that crowd out the point
    Cognitive demandDoes the task ask for the thinking the outcome names?Recall and definition items when the outcome says analyze or apply
    LoadIs anything here not helping learning?Long introductions, repeated summaries, decorative lists

    Here is a typical draft for an outcome that reads: "Students will evaluate whether a source is credible for a research paper."

    Source Credibility Practice 1. What does the "C" in the CRAAP test stand for? 2. Define "peer review." 3. True or false: .org websites are always reliable. 4. List three types of scholarly sources. 5. Name one database available through the library.
    Show the problem

    Every item is accurate, and none matches the outcome. All five ask students to recall terms. The outcome asks them to evaluate a source. A revised version would give students two real sources on the same topic and ask which is more credible for a specific paper, and why. Item 3 also teaches a shortcut that lateral reading research warns against: judging a site by its domain.

    Notice that this flaw is invisible to a fact check. Validation from Module 4 asks whether the content is true. Alignment asks whether it is the right content. You need both.

    5.3

    Accessibility and UDL checks

    CAST released version 3.0 of the UDL Guidelines in July 2024. Its central aim is learner agency: learners who are purposeful and reflective, resourceful and authentic, and strategic and action-oriented. AI can help you offer more ways in, such as a plain-language summary, a worked example, an audio version, or a set of practice items at different levels. It can also quietly add barriers. Run these checks on every AI-assisted material.

    CheckWhat to look forQuick fix
    HeadingsReal heading styles, not bold text that only looks like a headingApply Heading 1, 2, 3 in order
    Alt textEvery meaningful image has a description of its purpose; decorative images are marked decorativeWrite one or two sentences on what the image shows that matters
    Reading levelSentences and vocabulary match the audience; key terms are definedShorten sentences; define each term at first use
    ColorMeaning is never carried by color alone; contrast is sufficientAdd a label, icon, or pattern
    Captions and transcriptsVideo has accurate captions; audio has a transcriptReview auto-captions, especially names and technical terms
    Links and tablesLink text says where it goes; tables have header rowsReplace "click here"; mark the header row
    Review the handout below for accessibility. List: (1) any text that looks like a heading but is not marked as one, (2) every image and a draft alt text for each that states its purpose, (3) sentences over 25 words, with a shorter version, (4) any place where meaning depends on color alone. Do not rewrite the content otherwise. [paste handout]

    This prompt uses AI to find likely problems. Your institution's accessibility checker and your own review confirm them. Draft alt text in particular needs a human check, because only you know what the image is meant to teach.

    In practice

    CTEA welding instructor generates a plain-language version of a safety data sheet summary for students reading below grade level. They keep the original alongside it, check every hazard statement against the source sheet, and offer both so students can choose.

      5.5

      Representation and disclosure

      Models tend to repeat the most common patterns in their training data. In practice that means examples that default to the same names, places, jobs, and family structures, and images that default to the same faces. No single example is a problem. A whole handout of them tells some students the field is not about them.

      • Scan names, roles, and settings across all examples, not one at a time.
      • Check who is the expert and who needs help.
      • For images, check skin tone, age, disability, and gender across the set.
      • Ask colleagues and, where you can, students for review. Co-design with people who have disabilities and with multilingual learners strengthens inclusive materials.

      Then disclose. A short note tells students how the material was made and who stands behind it. It also models the transparency you will ask of them in Module 7.

      AI-use note: I drafted parts of this handout with an AI tool and then revised it for accuracy, alignment with our course outcomes, accessibility, and examples. I am responsible for its content. If you notice an error, please tell me.
      In practice

      Student supportA tutoring center generates scenario cards for a study-skills workshop. Review shows that every struggling student in the scenarios is a first-generation student. The staff rewrite the set so challenges and strengths are spread across varied students.

      Responsible use

      Do not paste licensed textbook chapters, publisher test banks, or colleagues' unpublished materials into an AI tool unless your license and your institution allow it. Use your own material, open materials, or public-domain sources.

      5.6

      Authorship and copyright questions

      Copyright questions about AI are active and unsettled in places. Avoid simple claims in either direction. Here is what the U.S. Copyright Office has said, and what to route to your institution.

      What the Copyright Office concluded (Part 2, January 2025)

      • Prompts alone do not give a user enough control to be the author of AI-generated output.
      • Human authorship can be protected where a person selects, coordinates, arranges, or modifies AI material in a creative way.
      • When your own copyrightable work goes into a tool and remains perceptible in the output, your contribution remains yours.
      • Using AI as an assistive tool does not by itself undermine protection of a work you author.

      Questions to route to your institution

      • Who owns course materials you create with or without AI under your contract or intellectual property policy?
      • Can you upload licensed or third-party content to an approved AI tool?
      • How should AI-assisted materials be labeled when shared in a repository or published?
      • What applies when students' work is used to create course materials?

      Fieldwork · about 60 minutes

      Apply it to your course (10–40–10)

      10min · review

      Reread the accessibility table in 5.3 and the copyright summary in 5.6. Run your institution's accessibility checker on one current course file to see what it reports.

      40min · apply
      1. Choose one classroom item: a handout, case, explanation, practice set, or visual.
      2. Name its outcome and the evidence it supports.
      3. Draft or revise it with AI help from public, open, or your own material.
      4. Revise substantively for alignment, accuracy, demand, load, and representation.
      5. Run the accessibility checks and fix what you find.
      6. Write instructor notes, a source list, and an AI-use disclosure.
      10min · share

      Post the item with its quality-assurance record. Review one peer's item using the three-part protocol from the studio.

      Judgment check · unscored practice

      Test your judgment

      Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

      Q1.An AI-drafted practice set for an outcome that says "students will analyze trends in a data table" contains ten accurate definition questions. What is the main problem?

      Question 1 options
      Best answer: B. Accuracy is not the issue. The items measure the wrong kind of thinking. An aligned set would give students a table and ask them to describe and explain a trend.
      • A. The stem says the questions are accurate. A fact check would pass them and leave the real problem in place.
      • C. Length is a minor concern. Five recall questions still measure the wrong skill.
      • D. Disclosure matters, but a disclosed set that measures recall is still misaligned.

      Q2.You add an AI-generated diagram to a slide. Which alt text is best?

      Question 2 options
      Best answer: C. Good alt text conveys what the image teaches. A student using a screen reader gets the same key information as a sighted student.
      • A. How the image was made and why it was added are not what it teaches. Disclosure belongs in a note, not in alt text.
      • B. Sending the reader elsewhere withholds the content the image gives sighted students.
      • D. Describing colors and shapes misses the content and relies on visual features the reader cannot use.

      Q3.A colleague says, "I wrote a really detailed prompt, so I own the copyright on the AI image." Based on the Copyright Office's 2025 report, what is the most accurate response?

      Question 3 options
      Best answer: D. The Office concluded that prompts alone do not give enough control over expressive elements. Human contributions such as creative modification or arrangement can still be protected. Route specific cases to your institution.
      • A. The Office specifically concluded that prompts, even detailed ones, do not by themselves make a user the author.
      • B. The report says human contributions in AI-assisted works, such as creative modification or arrangement, can be protected.
      • C. Fair use is a defense to infringement, not an exclusion from copyright. Educational materials are protected like other works.

      Q4.An AI-drafted set of six clinical scenarios features nurses who are all women and physicians who are all men. Nothing in the scenarios is factually wrong. What should you do?

      Question 4 options
      Best answer: A. Representation is checked across the set. Varying who holds which role keeps the content accurate while removing a stereotype that some students will notice.
      • B. Accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. The pattern teaches something you did not intend.
      • C. A note shifts the work to students and leaves the stereotype in place.
      • D. Starting over is unnecessary when targeted revision fixes the problem.

      Q5.Which material is ready to post to students?

      Question 5 options
      Best answer: C. This is the improve-not-generate standard: substantive human revision against outcome, accuracy, accessibility, and representation, plus transparency.
      • A. Disclosure does not replace review.
      • B. Proofreading and a quick comparison miss alignment, accuracy, and accessibility.
      • D. An AI self-report is not a check. Use your institution's accessibility checker and your own review.
