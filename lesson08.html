    Week 8 · Module 8

    Ethics, Equity, Human Agency, and Implementation

    Driving questionWhat should remain human, what can be delegated, and how will you know whether the implementation helped learning?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Ethics case clinicArtifact: Decision record + 30-day pilot

    Overview

    From principles to decisions you can defend

    Most faculty agree with the same principles: be fair, protect privacy, be transparent, keep humans accountable. The hard part is turning those words into a decision about a specific student, tool, and assignment on a Tuesday afternoon. This module makes ethics operational.

    You will work through five realistic cases, see which principles each choice supports or strains, and record your reasoning. Then you turn your portfolio into a bounded 30-day pilot with human checkpoints, data boundaries, evidence, and stop conditions. A small pilot that teaches you something is worth more than a broad mandate to "use AI."

    Predict before you read

    A colleague says, "Our AI use is ethical because our tool vendor says it is FERPA compliant." What is missing from that reasoning?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    Compliance claims are about a vendor's contract and data handling, and they still need institutional review. Even an approved tool can be used unfairly, opaquely, or for decisions that should stay human. Ethics covers what you do with the tool: who benefits, who is burdened, who knows, and who can override it. A vendor statement answers only part of the privacy question.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Analyze privacy, bias, accessibility, equity, autonomy, transparency, labor, intellectual property, and environmental trade-offs in an AI use. Analyze
    • Identify the stakeholders affected by a decision, including those not in the room. Analyze
    • Make and document a proportionate use decision with a stated rationale. Evaluate
    • Develop a 30-day pilot plan with human checkpoints, data boundaries, evidence, and stop conditions. Create
    • Plan a 30-day and 60–90-day review that leads to continue, revise, or stop. Create

    Study the material

    Ethics in practice and the pilot

    8.1

    Principles you can act on

    EDUCAUSE's 2025 AI Ethical Guidelines for higher education draw on the Belmont Report and name eight principles. Each one becomes useful when you turn it into a question you can answer about your own course.

    PrincipleQuestion to ask about your AI use
    BeneficenceDoes this actually help students learn or help me teach better, and how will I know?
    JusticeWho gets the benefit and who carries the burden? Does it widen any gap?
    Respect for autonomyCan students and colleagues understand, choose, and opt out without penalty?
    Transparency and explainabilityDo students know when and how AI is involved in what they receive or how they are evaluated?
    Accountability and responsibilityWho reviews AI output, and who answers for a wrong result? (It should be a person.)
    Privacy and data protectionWhat data goes in, where does it go, and is the tool approved for it?
    Nondiscrimination and fairnessDoes the output treat groups of students differently, for example by language background?
    Assessment of risks and benefitsAre the expected benefits worth the risks, given the stakes and the evidence?

    Two other frameworks point the same way. UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Teachers starts with a "human-centred mindset" and treats ethics of AI as its own dimension. NIST's Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) lists risks that map onto these questions, including confabulation, data privacy, harmful bias, intellectual property, human-AI configuration, and environmental impacts. Labor matters too: who does the extra review work, and whose work is being replaced or devalued?

    In practice

    HumanitiesA writing instructor considers AI-generated model essays. The justice question changes the design: the models now show several voices and dialects of strong writing, not one.

    STEMA physics instructor considers an AI tutor for problem sets. The autonomy question leads to a rule: the tutor gives hints, students may turn it off, and nothing it says affects grades.

    8.2

    Stakeholders, proportion, and the decision record

    Ethical analysis fails most often by leaving someone out. Before you decide, list who is affected: students in the course (and specific groups within it, such as multilingual students, students with disabilities, or students without reliable devices), teaching assistants, colleagues who share the course, support offices, and anyone whose data or creative work is involved.

    Then match safeguards to stakes. Drafting practice questions you will review is low stakes. Feedback students act on is moderate. Anything that affects grades, standing, or misconduct findings is high stakes and needs a person who reviews every case and can override the output.

    A decision record has five lines

    1. Decision: what you will and will not do.
    2. Stakeholders: who is affected, including who is not in the room.
    3. Tensions: which principles pull against each other.
    4. Rationale: why this choice is proportionate to the stakes and evidence.
    5. Revisit if: the signal that would change your mind.

    AI can help draft guidance, but its ethics advice needs the same checking as any other output. Here is a typical draft.

    Write a short checklist for faculty on using AI ethically when grading.
    1. Tell students in the syllabus how AI is used in grading. 2. Review every AI-suggested score or comment before it reaches a student. 3. If a tool is marketed as FERPA compliant, you may upload any student work to it. 4. Check feedback across student groups for differences in tone and focus. 5. An AI-detection score above 90% is sufficient evidence of misconduct. 6. Give students a way to ask for a human review.
    Reveal the errors

    Item 3 is wrong. Marketing language is not institutional approval. Student work goes only into tools your institution has approved for that data, under its contract and policy. Item 5 is wrong. Detector scores can be wrong, and research has found high false-positive rates for non-native English writers. A score is never sufficient evidence on its own. Items 1, 2, 4, and 6 are sound.

    Responsible use

    Final grades and misconduct decisions stay with a person who has reviewed the evidence. AI may support formative feedback; it does not decide consequential outcomes.

    8.4

    Design a 30-day pilot

    Your portfolio ends with a pilot, not a pledge. A pilot is small enough to stop and specific enough to produce evidence. Plan it around the problem you chose in Module 1.

    Pilot elementWhat to write
    Learning problemThe specific problem from Module 1 and the outcome it affects
    ParticipantsWhich section, how many students, and who is teaching or assisting
    Approved toolThe institution-approved tool, or a non-AI approach, and why it fits
    Human checkpointsWhere a person reviews output before it reaches students, and who that person is
    Data boundariesWhat data may and may not enter the tool
    Evidence of effectivenessWhat you will compare, such as before-and-after work samples, a short student survey, or your own time log
    Student communicationWhat you will tell students, when, and how they can ask questions or opt out
    Fallback planWhat happens if the tool is unavailable or a student uses the non-AI path
    Stop conditionsThe observable signals that pause or end the pilot
    In practice

    HealthA nursing instructor pilots AI-generated patient-case variations for one lab section. Stop condition: any clinical error that reaches students, or more than two students reporting that cases confused rather than helped.

    CTEAn automotive instructor pilots AI-simplified versions of service-manual procedures. Human checkpoint: every procedure is compared line by line with the manufacturer's manual before release.

    8.5

    Review, then continue, revise, or stop

    Treat AI literacy and AI use as things you keep improving, not a one-time compliance task. Plan two reviews now so that they happen.

    • At 30 days: Did the pilot launch? What changed in practice? What barriers appeared, such as access, time, or confusion?
    • At 60–90 days: Gather evidence of student experience, your workload, output quality, learning indicators, access problems, and unintended effects. Compare against your evidence plan.

    Then decide. Continue if the evidence supports it and no stop condition fired. Revise if the idea holds but the workflow, safeguards, or communication need work. Stop if the evidence is weak, the costs are high, or a stop condition fired. Stopping is a successful outcome of a well-run pilot; it means you learned something before scaling a mistake.

    Also look back at yourself. Retake the readiness inventory and choose "End of program." The portfolio page compares your starting point and end-of-program ratings, and it places your original artifact next to the redesigned one so you can see what changed in alignment, validation, transparency, inclusion, and feasibility.

    Judgment check · unscored practice

    Test your judgment on ethics and pilots

    Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

    Q1.A colleague's pilot plan says: "Stop if things are not going well." Which revision most improves it?

    Question 1 options
    Best answer: D. A stop condition has to be specific and observable so that it can trigger before harm accumulates. Writing it in advance prevents the sunk-cost habit of explaining problems away.
    • A. Running a harmful pilot to completion defeats the purpose of piloting.
    • B. Deciding whether to stop is an accountability decision that stays with a person, and a tool cannot judge its own failures.
    • C. End-of-term feedback is useful evidence, but it comes too late to protect current students.

    Q2.At the 30-day check, students say AI-drafted feedback was helpful, but you spent more time reviewing it than you used to spend writing feedback. What is the best next step?

    Question 2 options
    Best answer: B. One indicator at 30 days is not a verdict. The evidence shows value and a cost, so the proportionate move is to revise the workflow and keep measuring both before deciding.
    • A. Stopping discards a benefit students reported without testing whether the cost can be reduced.
    • C. Spot-checking lets unreviewed AI feedback reach students, which removes the human checkpoint.
    • D. Expanding on one month of satisfaction data skips the review that tells you whether it helped learning.

    Q3.Which statement is an operational ethics decision rather than a value statement?

    Question 3 options
    Best answer: C. It names an action, a time, a sample, the group at risk, and what happens if the check fails. That is a decision someone can carry out and review.
    • A. Fairness matters, but this sentence does not say what anyone will do.
    • B. This is a goal, not a decision.
    • D. A real value, but it does not tell you how to act in a specific case.

    Q4.A colleague asks whether the energy use of AI tools belongs in a course-level decision. What is the most defensible answer?

    Question 4 options
    Best answer: D. Environmental impact is a recognized risk area. Treating it as one factor among several keeps the analysis honest without turning it into an absolute rule.
    • A. Individual and institutional choices add up; NIST includes the risk in its profile for organizations.
    • B. Avoiding AI whenever any alternative exists turns one factor into an absolute rule and ignores the benefit side.
    • C. Exact figures are uncertain and change, but uncertainty is a reason to weigh it carefully, not to ignore it.

    Q5.During your pilot, a student declines to use the AI tool for privacy reasons. What should happen?

    Question 5 options
    Best answer: A. Respect for autonomy and justice require a real alternative without penalty. Opt-outs are also useful evidence about how acceptable the use is to students.
    • B. Requiring an AI tool removes a choice the program commits to preserving, and may conflict with institutional policy.
    • C. A penalty for declining makes the "choice" coercive.
    • D. Entering the student's work into the tool overrides the very concern they raised.
