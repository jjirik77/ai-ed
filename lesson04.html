    Week 4 · Module 4

    Output, Source, and Data Validation

    Driving questionWhat evidence is sufficient before an AI output can influence teaching or learning?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: TRACE red-teamArtifact: Validation audit

    Overview

    Fluent is not the same as verified

    A language model produces the most plausible next words. Plausible and true often overlap, which is why AI output is useful. They do not always overlap, which is why it needs checking. This module gives you a short, repeatable protocol for deciding when an output is ready to reach students.

    The protocol is called TRACE. You set the stakes, review each claim, check it against authoritative evidence, look for bias, privacy, and completeness problems, and then explain your final decision. The depth of checking scales with the stakes. A brainstormed list of discussion questions needs a quick read. A worked solution, a statistic on a slide, or a citation in a reading guide needs the full protocol.

    Predict before you read

    An AI tool gives you a paragraph with three citations. All three have real-looking authors, journals, and years. How many would you expect to exist exactly as cited? What would you check first?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    You cannot tell from the citation alone, which is the point. Fabricated references are formatted exactly like real ones. Check each one in a library database or the publisher site before you trust it, and then check that the source actually says what the paragraph claims. A real source that says something different is as much of a problem as a fake one.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Separate an AI output into claims, sources, interpretations, and recommendations. Analyze
    • Verify quotations, citations, and factual claims against authoritative sources using lateral reading. Evaluate
    • Test data and calculations independently instead of trusting the model's arithmetic. Apply
    • Identify bias, omissions, uncertainty, and fabricated evidence in an output. Analyze
    • Apply data-protection rules so that no protected information enters a non-approved tool. Apply

    Core ideas

    4.1

    Why fluent output still fails

    A model does not look facts up unless it is connected to search or to your documents. It generates text that fits the patterns of its training data. That process explains four common failures you will meet in this module.

    FailureWhat it looks likeWhy it happens
    Fabricated sourceA citation with a real-sounding author, journal, and year that does not exist, or exists but says something elseThe model has learned what citations look like, not which ones are real
    Numerical errorA percentage that does not match its own numbers; a unit conversion off by a factor of tenArithmetic is predicted as text unless the tool runs code
    Missing perspectiveA history told only from one region; a case study where every patient is the same age and backgroundTraining data overrepresents some voices and places
    Overconfident claim"Always," "proves," "the only cause," with no hedge or evidenceConfident phrasing is common in training text and reads as helpful

    The common thread is that the errors are not marked. Fabricated citations are formatted perfectly. A wrong percentage sits in a grammatical sentence. That is why validation is a habit you apply to every consequential output, not a reaction to something that looks wrong.

    Responsible use

    Automation bias grows under time pressure. If you are checking output at 11 p.m. before a class, lower the stakes of what you use or postpone it. A tired reviewer is the weakest link in the protocol.

    4.2

    The TRACE protocol

    StepQuestions to askTypical action
    T Task and stakesWhat will this output be used for? Who is harmed if it is wrong? Is it low, moderate, or high stakes?Choose the depth of checking
    R Review claimsWhich sentences are checkable claims? Which are sources, interpretations, or recommendations?Highlight every claim, number, quotation, and citation
    A Authoritative evidenceDoes an accountable source confirm each claim? Does the cited source exist and say this?Read laterally; recompute numbers yourself
    C Check bias, privacy, completenessWhose perspective is missing? Is any example stereotyped? Did any protected data go in or come out?Add, reframe, or remove
    E Explain the decisionWhat did you change and why? What remains uncertain?Record use, revise, or reject with a reason
    4.3

    Lateral reading and source tracing

    Wineburg and McGrew (2019) watched 10 professional fact checkers, 10 PhD historians, and 25 Stanford undergraduates evaluate unfamiliar websites. The historians and students tended to read vertically. They stayed on a site and judged it by its design, its "About" page, and its tone. The fact checkers left the site almost immediately, opened new tabs, and checked what other sources said about the organization and its claim. They reached better-supported conclusions in less time.

    The same move works on AI output. Do not ask the model whether its own answer is correct. That is vertical reading with extra steps. Instead, leave the output:

    1. Search for the source, not the claim. Look up the cited article in your library database or a scholarly search engine by title and author.
    2. Open the source and find the passage. Confirm that it says what the output claims, with the same scope and the same numbers.
    3. Check the consensus. For a contested or surprising claim, find two independent authoritative sources.
    4. Recompute. Redo every calculation in a spreadsheet or by hand. Check units and denominators.
    List every factual claim, statistic, and citation in the text below as a numbered table with three columns: claim, type (fact, number, citation, interpretation), and what source would confirm it. Do not judge whether the claims are true. [paste the output here]

    This prompt uses AI for the part it does well, which is structuring text, and leaves the judging to you and to authoritative sources. It is a useful start to the Review step.

    In practice

    HealthA nursing instructor asks for a medication dosage example. The output uses mg where the scenario requires mcg, a thousandfold error, in a correctly formatted calculation. Recomputing with units written out catches it in under a minute.

    HumanitiesA literature instructor asks for a quotation from a public-domain novel to anchor a discussion. The quotation sounds right but does not appear in the text. A search of a digital edition shows the closest real passage, which is better for discussion anyway.

    4.4

    Practice: red-team an AI mini-lesson

    The mini-lesson below was written for this course to mimic a typical AI draft. It contains exactly four planted errors, one of each type from 4.1, and five sound lines. Work through TRACE: set the stakes, flag and classify lines, read what lateral checking finds, then make and explain a decision.

      Notice how few lines carried the errors and how ordinary they looked. In live sessions, most teams find the numerical error and the fabricated citation quickly. The missing perspective is found least often, because an omission does not appear on the page.

      4.5

      Data validation and data protection

      "Data validation" covers two different jobs. The first is checking data that AI produced or analyzed. The second is protecting data you might put into an AI tool. Both belong in TRACE.

      When AI produces or analyzes data

      • Check that every number traces back to a source dataset you can open.
      • Recompute totals, percentages, and averages. Check the denominator.
      • Check that a chart's axes, scale, and labels match the data. A truncated axis can exaggerate a small difference.
      • If the tool ran code, read the code or at least the steps. If it did not run code, assume arithmetic beyond simple sums needs rechecking.

      What must never go into a non-approved tool

      Responsible use
      • Identifiable student work, names, ID numbers, grades, or accommodations
      • Health, counseling, disability, or disciplinary records
      • Unpublished research data, manuscripts under review, or grant proposals you are reviewing
      • Confidential institutional, personnel, or financial information

      Use fictional, public, or properly de-identified material when practicing. Removing a name is not always enough: a unique combination of course, date, and detail can still identify a student.

      Fieldwork · about 60 minutes

      Apply it to your course (10–40–10)

      10min · review

      Reread the TRACE table in 4.2 and the never-paste list in 4.5. Skim one lateral-reading example from your library's research guide.

      40min · apply
      1. Pick an AI output relevant to your course: one you made, or one you generate now from public or fictional material.
      2. Set the stakes and write one sentence explaining them.
      3. Number every claim, figure, quotation, and citation.
      4. Mark each as verified, revised, removed, or unresolved, and record the evidence.
      5. Write a three-sentence explanation of your final decision.
      10min · share

      Bring your audit to the next session or send it to a peer. Comment on one peer's audit: name a claim you would check further or an omission you notice.

      Judgment check · unscored practice

      Test your judgment

      Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

      Q1.An AI tool drafts a one-page reading guide for your course. It cites a 2021 article that you find in your library database under the same title and authors. What should you do before using the citation?

      Question 1 options
      Best answer: C. Existence is only half of source verification. Models often attach real sources to claims those sources do not make. The passage itself is the evidence.
      • A. Finding the article rules out a fabricated reference but not a misattributed claim.
      • B. The model can generate a plausible quotation that is not in the article. Only opening the article confirms it.
      • D. Replacing a source you have not checked with one you know may be fine, but it skips the habit this case is testing and may lose a relevant source.

      Q2.A colleague wants AI to summarize patterns in last term's discussion posts to improve the course. The posts include student names. Which approach best protects students?

      Question 2 options
      Best answer: A. Student work is an educational record. It belongs only in tools your institution has approved for that data class, and removing identifiers reduces risk further. Policy comes before the upload, not after.
      • B. Removing names helps, but distinctive details can still identify students, and a non-approved tool is still the wrong place for records.
      • C. Telling students after the fact does not undo a disclosure.
      • D. History and training settings on a personal account do not make it an approved tool or create institutional data protections.

      Q3.An AI-generated slide says enrollment in a program "rose 40%, from 250 to 300 students." What is the most efficient first check?

      Question 3 options
      Best answer: B. The output contradicts itself: 250 to 300 is a 20% increase. Internal arithmetic is the fastest check and catches the error before you spend time on sources.
      • A. You will still need the report to confirm the base numbers, but recomputing first shows the claim is already wrong.
      • C. A source request can return a plausible but fabricated reference.
      • D. Removing it may be premature. The corrected figure may still be useful once verified.

      Q4.You ask AI for three case studies of workplace conflict for a management course. All three feature managers with similar names and backgrounds, and all are resolved by the manager acting alone. Which TRACE step addresses this most directly?

      Question 4 options
      Best answer: C. Nothing in the cases is false, so claim checking will not catch it. The problem is a narrow and repetitive representation of who manages and how conflict is resolved, which is a completeness and bias issue.
      • A. Stakes set how carefully you check, but they do not identify this problem.
      • B. Claim review looks for checkable facts; these are fictional cases with no false facts.
      • D. Authoritative evidence confirms facts, and the cases contain no false facts to confirm.

      Q5.You are short on time before class. An AI-drafted set of ten review questions for an ungraded warm-up looks reasonable. Which response fits TRACE?

      Question 5 options
      Best answer: D. TRACE scales with stakes. Ungraded practice is low stakes, but a wrong answer key still teaches something false. Answering the questions yourself is a fast, sufficient check.
      • A. Low stakes reduce the depth of checking, not the need for it. Students still learn from the answer key.
      • B. Full protocol is more than the stakes require and can make AI use slower than writing the questions yourself.
      • C. Error-finding can be a strong activity when designed and announced as one, not as a way to skip your own review.
