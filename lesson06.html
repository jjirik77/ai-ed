    Week 6 · Module 6

    Student AI Literacy and Learning Activities

    Driving questionHow can students learn with AI while still doing the cognitive work that the course intends to develop?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Responsibility mapArtifact: Student activity

    Overview

    Use is not the same as literacy

    Most of your students already use AI. Far fewer know when it helps their learning and when it quietly does the learning for them. This module shows you how to design activities where AI has a small, clear job and students still do the thinking your course exists to build.

    The key idea is the bounded role. AI can act as a coach, a simulator, a debate partner, a critic, or a source of flawed work. Each role works only when you decide in advance which steps belong to the student, which belong to AI, and which belong to you. You will map those steps, find where the thinking leaks away, and add a short debrief so students can see their own reasoning.

    Predict before you read

    A student uses AI to explain a hard reading, then writes a summary that is clear and accurate. Did the student learn the reading? What would you need to see to know?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    You cannot tell from the summary alone. A clear summary shows that a clear explanation existed, not that the student built the understanding. You would need evidence of the student's own reasoning: a question they asked, a point where they disagreed with the explanation, a connection to another reading, or a short oral explanation. Designing for that evidence is the core move in this module.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Design an activity in which AI has a bounded role such as coach, simulator, debate partner, critic, or source of flawed work. Create
    • Locate the steps in an activity where productive thinking could be outsourced to AI. Analyze
    • Teach students to prompt, question, verify, revise, disclose, and reflect on their AI use. Apply
    • Preserve non-AI alternatives and equitable access for every required activity. Apply
    • Collect evidence of student reasoning, not only finished products. Evaluate

    Study the material

    Core ideas

    6.1

    Students use AI; many do not feel ready

    In July 2024 the Digital Education Council surveyed 3,839 students in 16 countries. Eighty-six percent said they regularly use AI in their studies, and 54% use it weekly. Yet 58% said they lack sufficient AI knowledge and skills, and 48% did not feel prepared for an AI-enabled workplace. High use with low confidence is the gap this module addresses.

    Students rarely need help opening a chat window. They need help with judgment: when to use AI, what to ask, how to check what comes back, and how to tell whether they learned anything. Those are teachable skills, and they are best taught inside your discipline, on real course tasks.

    Start from your learning outcome, not from the tool. Ask which thinking the outcome names. If the outcome is "construct an argument from primary sources," then constructing the argument is the work students must do. AI can still help with other parts, such as generating counterarguments to test against, or quizzing students on source details.

    In practice

    HumanitiesA philosophy instructor asks students to write their own objection to an argument first. Only then do they ask AI for the strongest objection it can produce and compare the two. The student's objection is the evidence; the AI objection is the test.

    Student supportA tutoring center coach shows students how to ask AI to quiz them on lecture notes instead of summarizing the notes. Retrieval practice stays with the student.

    6.2

    Five bounded roles for AI

    RoleWhat AI doesWhat the student doesWatch for
    CoachAsks guiding questions and gives hintsAttempts first, explains reasoning, decides next stepCoach slides into giving the answer
    SimulatorPlays a patient, client, customer, or historical figureInterviews, observes, makes and justifies decisionsSimulated details that are inaccurate or stereotyped
    Debate partnerArgues the opposing sideBuilds a position, rebuts, checks AI's claimsFluent but unsupported counterarguments
    CriticGives feedback on a student draft against a rubricDecides which feedback to accept and whyStudents accepting all feedback without judgment
    Source of flawed workProduces an answer, procedure, or essay with errorsFinds, explains, and fixes the errorsErrors students cannot yet detect; check the level

    Roles drift. A coach that is asked the same question twice may simply give the answer. Test every role yourself before students do, and write the role into the prompt students use. The example below shows what drift looks like.

    I am a student in an introductory calculus course. Act as a tutor. Help me reason, but do not give me final answers. Ask one question at a time. If I make a mistake, ask me to check that step instead of correcting it. If I ask you to just give me the answer, remind me that I need to show my own reasoning.
    Student: I need the derivative of f(x) = x² sin x. Can you just tell me? Tutor: Great question! Use the product rule. f′(x) = 2x sin x + x² sin x. So the answer is 2x sin x + x² sin x. Want me to do the next problem for you too?
    Show the errors

    Two problems. First, the role failed: the tutor gave the full answer and offered to do the next problem, which is exactly what the prompt ruled out. Second, the math is wrong. The derivative of sin x is cos x, so the second term should be x² cos x. A student who trusted the tutor would learn a wrong result. Role constraints reduce drift but do not prevent it, which is why the activity still needs a student-owned check.

    Responsible use

    Simulators can reproduce stereotypes about patients, clients, or cultures. Review generated personas before class and ask students to note anything that seems inaccurate or one-dimensional as part of the activity.

    6.3

    Map who does the thinking

    The AI–Student–Instructor responsibility map is a simple design tool. List the steps of your activity. For each step, decide who owns it. Then mark which steps carry the thinking your outcome names. If any of those steps belongs to AI, the activity is outsourcing the learning.

    Use the interactive below to try it. Pick an activity, assign each step, and watch the outsourcing-risk and evidence meters. The starting map is the tempting version many first drafts look like.

    6.4

    Teach the student routine and the debrief

    Students need a routine they can repeat across courses. Teach it explicitly, model it once, and ask for evidence of each step.

    StepWhat students doEvidence you can ask for
    PromptState the goal, context, and the role AI should playThe prompt they used
    QuestionPush back, ask for reasons, ask what is uncertainOne follow-up question and why they asked it
    VerifyCheck claims against course sourcesOne claim checked, with the source
    ReviseChange their own work based on what held upA before and after, or a tracked change
    DiscloseState what AI did, using the course formatA short AI-use statement
    ReflectExplain how their thinking changedTwo or three sentences in their own words

    The debrief is where students see their own thinking. Keep it short and specific. Three questions work well in most disciplines:

    1. Where did you disagree with the AI, and what evidence settled it?
    2. Which part of this task did you do without AI, and why that part?
    3. What would you do differently next time?
    In practice

    HealthNursing students interview an AI-simulated patient, then prioritize interventions. In the debrief, each student names one detail the "patient" gave that did not fit the clinical picture and how they checked it.

    CTEAutomotive students ask AI to explain a diagnostic trouble code, then confirm the explanation against the service manual before touching the vehicle. The debrief asks what the manual said that the AI did not.

    6.5

    Access, choice, and the no-AI fallback

    Every required AI activity needs an equivalent path that meets the same outcome without AI. Some students cannot afford paid tools. Some have privacy, religious, or professional reasons not to create accounts. Some use assistive technology that a tool does not support. The fallback should be a real alternative, assessed on the same outcome, not a lesser assignment.

    Build the activity from eight parts. If one is missing, the activity is not ready.

    1. Learning outcome the activity serves
    2. AI role, bounded and written into the prompt
    3. Student role, including the target thinking
    4. Verification step the student performs
    5. Evidence-of-learning requirement you will review
    6. Disclosure expectation in the course format
    7. Accessibility provision, such as screen-reader-compatible materials and extra time
    8. No-AI fallback that meets the same outcome
    In practice

    STEMIn a statistics activity, AI generates a messy practice dataset. The no-AI fallback is a prepared dataset with the same planted problems. Both groups do the same cleaning and interpretation, so evidence is comparable.

    Responsible use

    Never require students to create personal accounts on a non-approved tool or to enter personal information to complete coursework. If the institution has no approved tool for students, run the AI step as an instructor demonstration with pre-captured outputs.

    Live studio · 60 minutes

    Session run sheet

    MinutesMoveWhat happens
    0–5Check-inPoll: "In your course, which student task is most likely to be handed to AI right now?" Two or three responses aloud.
    5–15ConceptUse versus literacy, the five bounded roles, and the role-drift example in 6.2. Contrast an activity where AI writes the draft with one where AI argues the other side.
    15–25Faculty as studentsParticipants complete a short debate-partner activity as students: write a position, ask AI for the strongest objection, verify one claim, revise. Facilitator times it tightly.
    25–43RedesignTeams map the activity they just did using W6, mark where thinking was productive and where it could be outsourced, then redesign one of their own course activities with a metacognitive debrief.
    43–53Peer critiquePairs swap maps. Protocol: "Which step carries your outcome? Who owns it? What evidence will you see?"
    53–58CommitmentEach participant names the activity they will build this week and its no-AI fallback.
    58–60Exit ticket"Where in your activity could a student still skip the thinking, and how would you know?"

    Fieldwork · about 60 minutes

    Apply it to your course (10–40–10)

    10min · review

    Reread the eight-part checklist in 6.5 and the student routine in 6.4. Look at the debrief questions and adapt one to your discipline.

    40min · apply
    1. Pick a 20 to 40 minute activity in your course.
    2. Write the outcome, the AI role, and the student role.
    3. Map each step to AI, student, or instructor; mark the target thinking.
    4. Add a verification step, an evidence requirement, a disclosure line, an accessibility provision, and a no-AI fallback.
    5. Test the AI role yourself twice, including asking for the answer outright.
    10min · share

    Post your activity and facilitation notes. Review one peer's map and name the step you think is most at risk of being outsourced.

    Judgment check · unscored practice

    Test your judgment

    Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

    Q1.A history instructor tells students: "Use AI to write a first draft of your argument, then revise it." The outcome is "construct a historical argument from primary sources." What is the biggest problem?

    Question 1 options
    Best answer: D. The outcome is constructing an argument. Revising someone else's argument, even well, is editing. Move AI to a bounded role such as critic or debate partner after the student drafts.
    • A. Students may learn some prompting, but the bigger issue is that the history thinking is skipped.
    • B. Length and generic style are easy to fix and are not the design flaw.
    • C. Disclosure matters, but clear disclosure of an outsourced draft still leaves the outcome unassessed.

    Q2.Your outcome is "identify weaknesses in a laboratory procedure." Which AI role fits best?

    Question 2 options
    Best answer: A. Source of flawed work aligns directly with the outcome. Students do the identifying and explaining, and you can check the flaws they found against the ones planted.
    • B. Following a model procedure does not practice finding weaknesses.
    • C. Here AI does the identifying, which is the thinking the outcome names. Students only make the fixes.
    • D. A summary may help review but does not target the outcome.

    Q3.Several students say they will not create an account on any AI tool for privacy reasons. What should you do?

    Question 3 options
    Best answer: B. Choice and access are part of responsible design. An approved tool reduces privacy risk, and a real fallback lets students meet the outcome either way.
    • A. Requiring consumer accounts can expose student data and ignores legitimate reasons to opt out.
    • C. Excusing them removes the learning, not just the tool.
    • D. Pairing can work as one option, but it makes the student dependent on a peer and does not respect their choice by default.

    Q4.Which debrief question gives you the strongest evidence that a student did their own thinking?

    Question 4 options
    Best answer: D. Disagreement plus evidence shows judgment the student exercised. It is short to read and hard to fake without engaging with the task.
    • A. Students can answer this by restating the AI output. It does not show their own judgment.
    • B. Full logs are long to review, raise privacy questions, and show what happened, not what the student understood.
    • C. A self-estimated percentage is hard to verify and says nothing about the student's reasoning.

    Q5.While testing your activity, you find that the AI "tutor" gives full answers when asked twice. What is the best response?

    Question 5 options
    Best answer: A. Role drift is expected. Designing so that students commit their own thinking first, and checking that thinking, keeps the learning with the student even when the tool slips.
    • B. Dropping AI is a defensible choice, but the problem can usually be designed around while keeping the benefit.
    • C. A rule alone does not change the design and is hard to follow under deadline pressure.
    • D. Any tool can drift. One test is not enough to trust a new tool.
