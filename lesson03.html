    Week 3 · Module 3

    Prompt and Workflow Engineering

    Driving questionHow can you turn a one-off chat into a repeatable, reviewable workflow?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Prompt canvasArtifact: Reusable workflow

    Overview

    A prompt is a specification, not a spell

    Most weak AI output starts with a weak request. "Make some practice problems" leaves the model to guess the course, the level, the format, and what counts as good. It guesses in the most generic direction. This module treats a prompt the way you would treat directions to a new teaching assistant: say what you want, why, from what material, within what limits, in what form, and how it will be judged.

    You will learn an eight-part Prompt Specification Canvas, use it to rebuild a weak prompt, and then iterate based on a diagnosed failure instead of rewording at random. The goal is a reusable workflow you can run again next term. A good prompt improves the first draft. It does not remove the need to check it.

    Predict before you read

    You ask an AI tool to "write five quiz questions on the causes of World War I." The questions come back too easy and all multiple choice. What would you add to the prompt to fix both problems?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    Name the audience and the cognitive demand, and specify the format. For example: "for second-year history majors who have read three primary sources; two questions should ask students to compare sources; mix two short-answer and three multiple-choice items." The model cannot infer level or format from the topic alone. Phrases like "be thorough" or "act as an expert" do much less than these concrete details.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Write a task specification that includes goal, context, source material, constraints, process, output format, quality criteria, and validation instructions. Create
    • Use examples and rubrics inside a prompt to show the model what good looks like. Apply
    • Diagnose why an output failed and trace the failure to a missing or unclear part of the prompt. Analyze
    • Iterate on a prompt by fixing the diagnosed cause and testing an edge case. Create
    • Package a tested prompt as a reusable workflow with an iteration log and a human-review statement. Create

    Core ideas

    3.1

    Prompting is specification plus evaluation

    Lists of "magic phrases" circulate widely. Some help a little. None replace the information the model is missing. A model generates the most plausible response to the words it receives. If the words do not mention your students, your outcome, or your rubric, the response cannot reflect them.

    Studies of structured prompting point in the same direction. Clear, contextual, task-specific prompts tend to reduce misinterpretation and improve results on the task. They do not make the output trustworthy. That is why this course pairs every prompt with a check, using the TRACE protocol from Module 4.

    Common habitWhy it disappointsBetter move
    "Act as an expert professor"A role sets tone but carries no facts about your courseState the course, level, and outcome
    "Be detailed and accurate"Every model already tries to sound detailed and accurateGive quality criteria you will check
    Rewording until it worksRandom changes hide what actually fixed the problemName the failure, change one element, retest
    One long chat for everythingEarlier turns drift into later answersStart a fresh session per task with a saved prompt
    3.2

    The Prompt Specification Canvas

    ElementAsk yourselfExample phrase
    GoalWhat will I do with this?"Practice problems for an ungraded warm-up before Thursday's lab"
    ContextWho are the learners and what do they know?"First-year students; they have seen the formula once but not applied it"
    Source materialWhat should it draw from?"Use only the attached lecture notes and the definitions in them"
    ConstraintsWhat limits apply?"Under 300 words; no proper names; reading level about grade 10"
    Desired processIn what order should it work?"First list the misconceptions, then write one item for each"
    Output formatWhat shape do I need?"A numbered list, then an answer key with one-line explanations"
    Quality criteriaHow will I judge it?"Each item targets one outcome; distractors reflect real errors"
    ValidationWhat should it flag for me?"Mark any fact you are unsure of with [CHECK]; I will verify every answer"
    3.3

    Worked example: diagnose, then rebuild

    A business ethics instructor wants a short case for a week on conflicts of interest. Here is the first attempt.

    Write a case study about ethics in business.
    Case Study: The Ethical Dilemma at TechCorp Sarah, a mid-level manager at TechCorp, discovers that her company has been cutting corners on product safety. She must decide whether to report the issue to regulators, risking her job, or stay silent. Discussion: What should Sarah do? What ethical principles apply?

    Nothing here is false, and nothing here is usable. Diagnose before rewriting. The case is generic because the prompt gave no context. It is about whistleblowing, not conflicts of interest, because the prompt gave no goal. It has one obvious answer because there were no quality criteria asking for genuine tension. And it is too short to support a 30-minute discussion because there was no format.

    Goal: Write a discussion case for a 30-minute small-group activity on conflicts of interest. Context: Second-year undergraduate business students. They have read a short chapter that defines actual, potential, and perceived conflicts of interest. Many work part time in retail or food service. Source material: Use only the three definitions pasted below. [paste definitions] Constraints: 350 to 450 words. Set it in a mid-sized regional organization, not a technology company. No real company or person names. Avoid a single obviously right answer. Process: First decide which of the three conflict types the case will show and how a reasonable person could defend each choice. Then write the case. Format: Title; the case in 3 to 4 paragraphs; then 4 discussion questions that move from identifying to evaluating; then a one-paragraph facilitator note on the intended tension. Quality criteria: The conflict is subtle enough that groups disagree. Each discussion question requires students to apply a definition from the reading. Validation: Do not add statistics, laws, or citations. If you rely on any general claim about business practice, mark it [CHECK].

    The rebuilt prompt is longer, but every line does work. Save it. Next term, you change the definitions and the setting and run it again. That is the difference between a chat and a workflow.

    In practice

    STEMA chemistry instructor adds one example of a strong practice problem and one weak one to the prompt, with a sentence on why each is strong or weak. Examples often fix level and style faster than any description.

    Student supportAn advising coordinator turns a long financial-aid policy into a student FAQ. The prompt names the reading level, tells the model to quote the policy for every deadline, and to say "ask an advisor" when the policy is silent.

      3.5
      Iterate on diagnosed failure
      

      When an output disappoints, name the failure before you touch the prompt. Then change the element that controls it, and change only that. If you change five things at once, you will not know which one helped, and you cannot reuse the lesson.
      

      Observed failureLikely missing elementTargeted fix
      

      Too easy or too hardContextState level, prior knowledge, and an example item at the right level
      

      Off-topic or genericGoal or source materialSay what it is for; paste the material to draw from
      

      Invented facts or citationsConstraints and validation"Use only the material provided; mark anything else [CHECK]"
      

      Wrong shape or lengthOutput formatSpecify sections, order, and word range
      

      Plausible but shallowQuality criteriaAdd a short rubric or a strong example
      

      Skips steps or reasoningDesired processList the steps in order
      

      

      Then test an edge case. Run the prompt on an unusual input, such as a very short reading, a topic outside the usual unit, or a student question that is partly off-topic. A prompt that works only on the example you wrote it for is not ready to reuse.
      

      One trap deserves its own warning. Asking the model to "check your work" can improve some outputs, but its self-report is not evidence.
      

      Here are your 3 practice problems with answers.

1. A sample of 36 has mean 72 and standard deviation 12. Find the standard error.
   Answer: SE = 12 / 6 = 2

2. Convert 2.5 kilometers to meters.
   Answer: 2.5 x 100 = 250 meters

3. A class has 18 students who passed out of 24. What percent passed?
   Answer: 18 / 24 = 75%

Self-check: I verified all three answers. All are correct.
      

      Show the error
      Problem 2 is wrong: 1 kilometer is 1,000 meters, so the answer is 2,500 meters. The self-check line is just more generated text. Only your own recalculation counts as a check.
      

      Responsible use
      Reusable workflows often grow to include real course material. Keep student work, grades, and other protected data out of any tool your institution has not approved for that data. When a workflow handles student work, write the de-identification step into the workflow itself so it is not forgotten.
      

      3.6
      From chat to reusable workflow
      

      A workflow is a saved sequence you can run again: the prompt, the inputs it needs, the human checks between steps, and a statement of what a person must review before anything reaches students. Keep it in a document, not only in a chat history that may disappear or be hard to find.
      

      1. Input: what you paste in (for example, a reading, outcomes, or a rubric), with any de-identification step.
        2. 

      2. Prompt: the tested specification, with placeholders in brackets.
        3. 

      3. Check: a check proportionate to the stakes, and who does it (Module 4 gives you the TRACE protocol).
        4. 

      4. Revise: what you typically edit by hand, such as examples or tone.
        5. 

      5. Log: version, date, what failed, what changed.
      

      Human review statement
      End every workflow with one sentence such as: "Before use, I verify every answer, check alignment with outcome 3, and replace any example that does not fit my students." It is a promise to yourself and a signal to any colleague who borrows the workflow.
      

      In practice
      CTEAn automotive instructor builds a three-step workflow: summarize a service bulletin at a grade 10 level, generate five diagnostic scenario questions from it, then list every torque value and part number for the instructor to check against the bulletin. The third step exists only to make validation fast.
      HumanitiesA history instructor saves a workflow that turns a primary-source excerpt into three sourcing questions. The iteration log shows that adding "do not summarize the document for students" was the change that kept the reading work with students.

      Foundation path
      Build one prompt for one low-stakes task. Use goal, context, constraints, format, and quality criteria, and log one iteration.
      Extension path
      Build a two- or three-step workflow in which a later step checks or transforms an earlier output. Add an example of good output to the prompt and test two edge cases.
      

      

      Judgment check · unscored practice
      Test your judgment
      Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.
      Q1.A colleague's prompt reads: "You are a world-class biology professor. Write excellent review questions on cell respiration." The questions are accurate but far too advanced for a community college intro course. What is the best fix?
      Question 1 options
      Best answer: A. The failure is level, which is controlled by context. A course description and a sample item tell the model what "right level" means. The expert role may even push the output toward advanced content.
      • B. "Easier" is vague. The model has to guess how much easier and in what way.
      • C. Generating extra and filtering can work but wastes time and does not produce a reusable prompt.
      • D. The problem is the specification, not the tool. A different tool given the same prompt faces the same guess.
      Q2.Your prompt produces a strong reading guide for a long chapter. On a two-page article, it invents three sections that the article does not have. Which response best follows the iteration method?
      Question 2 options
      Best answer: B. You found an edge case and diagnosed the failure: the format demanded sections the source could not supply, so the model filled them. A targeted constraint and an explicit "say so" path fixes the cause and keeps the prompt reusable.
      • A. Starting over discards what already works and hides what fixed the problem.
      • C. Avoiding the edge case leaves the workflow fragile and limits its use.
      • D. A general instruction like this does not tell the model what to do instead when content is missing.
      Q3.Which element is most likely to reduce invented citations in a reading list request?
      Question 3 options
      Best answer: D. Limiting the model to sources you supply, and giving it a way to flag what it cannot support, targets the cause. You still verify every item, as Module 4 will show.
      • A. A goal helps relevance but does not limit where references come from.
      • B. A detailed citation format makes fabricated references look more convincing, not less likely.
      • C. The request adds no mechanism. Without sources to check against, the model cannot tell real references from invented ones.
      Q4.A department wants to share one feedback-drafting prompt across twelve sections. Which addition matters most before sharing?
      Question 4 options
      Best answer: A. Shared workflows multiply both benefit and risk. The review statement keeps the instructor accountable, de-identification protects students, and naming the approved tool keeps records out of unapproved services.
      • B. A role adds tone, not safety or quality control.
      • C. Sounding personal without being reviewed can mislead students about who wrote the feedback.
      • D. Moving from feedback to grading raises the stakes and is addressed in Module 7. It should not be added casually.
      Q5.You ask a model to solve and then "double-check" ten practice problems. It reports that all answers are verified. What should you conclude?
      Question 5 options
      Best answer: B. A model's report that it checked is not evidence that it did, or that the check was correct. The validation element helps the model flag uncertainty, but the human check is what counts.
      • A. The claim of verification is itself an unverified output.
      • C. Spot-checking one of ten may be too little when students will study from the answer key.
      • D. Agreement between runs is not independent verification. The same model can repeat the same error.
