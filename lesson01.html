    Week 1 · Module 1

    AI Fundamentals and Human-Centered Use

    Driving questionWhere can AI improve learning or faculty work without displacing the essential human contribution?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractives: Card sort + next-tokenArtifact: Opportunity-risk map

    Overview

    A durable mental model beats a product tour

    Tools change every few months. The way they work changes much more slowly. This module gives you a plain-language model of what AI is, how a large language model produces text, and why that process makes some uses safe and others risky. With that model, you can predict how a tool will fail before it fails in front of students.

    You will also start the project that runs through all eight weeks. You will map where AI might help in one real course, where it could cause harm, and what must stay under your control. Then you will choose one low- or moderate-stakes teaching problem to investigate. Deciding that AI is not the right fit for that problem is a valid result.

    Predict before you read

    You ask a chatbot the same question twice, word for word, and get two different answers. Is the tool broken? What does the difference tell you about how it works?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    The tool is working as designed. A language model computes probabilities for the next piece of text and then samples from them, so two runs can take different paths. That is useful for brainstorming and a warning for facts. If an answer can change on a second try, a single fluent answer is not evidence that it is correct.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Distinguish AI, machine learning, generative AI, and rule-based automation using examples from your own work. Analyze
    • Describe training and inference, tokens, and context at a conceptual level. Understand
    • Explain why language-model output is probabilistic and what limitations follow from that. Understand
    • Map opportunities and risks for AI across one of your courses. Analyze
    • Select one course problem worth investigating and define what must stay under human control. Evaluate

    1.1

    AI, machine learning, generative AI, and automation

    "AI" gets used for almost any software that seems smart. That vagueness causes real problems. A rule-based reminder email and a chatbot that writes feedback carry very different risks, and they need different checks. The figure below shows how the terms nest.

    Most of this course focuses on generative AI and LLMs, because that is where faculty face the newest decisions. The other categories still matter.     1.2

    How a language model produces text

    A large language model works in two phases. In training, it processes a very large body of text and adjusts billions of internal settings, called weights, so that it gets better at predicting the next token. In inference, the finished model takes your prompt, breaks it into tokens, and generates a reply one token at a time. Each new token is chosen from a probability distribution over possible next tokens.

    Three ideas from this pipeline explain most of what you will see in later modules:

    • Probabilistic output. The model picks likely continuations. Likely is often correct, but a common error can be more likely than a rare truth.
    • Knowledge cutoff. Training data stops at a date. Unless the tool searches the web or your documents, it does not know about anything after that date, including your institution's newest policy.
    • Context. The prompt, any files you attach, and the conversation so far are all the model has to work with in that moment. Better context usually produces better output, which is the basis of Module 3.

    1.3

    Fluency is not truth

    Language models are trained to produce text that reads well. Readable, confident text feels trustworthy, and that feeling is the main trap. The comparison below comes from a typical request for a short science explanation.

    Explain why Earth has seasons. Write for 9th-grade students in about 80 words.
    Earth has seasons because its distance from the Sun changes throughout the year. In summer, Earth's orbit brings it closer to the Sun, so sunlight is stronger and days feel warmer. In winter, Earth moves farther away, so less heat reaches the surface. This is why the Northern and Southern Hemispheres both enjoy summer at the same time of year. Understanding our orbit helps explain the rhythm of the seasons.
    Show the errors

    The whole explanation rests on a common misconception. Seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, which changes how directly sunlight strikes each hemisphere. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during Northern Hemisphere winter. The hemispheres also have opposite seasons, not the same season. The paragraph is well organized, age-appropriate, and wrong in every sentence that matters. A misconception that appears often in everyday writing can be a likely continuation even when it is false.

    Now compare a less polished answer a teaching assistant might jot down:

    Accurate but rough

    "Seasons = tilt, not distance. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees. When your half leans toward the Sun, light hits more directly and days are longer, so it's summer. Other half gets winter at the same time. We're actually closest to the Sun in January."

    The rough answer needs editing. The fluent answer needs replacing. That is the core habit of this course: judge an output by checking its claims, not by how finished it sounds.

    Limits to keep in mind

    LimitationWhat it means for your teaching
    ConfabulationInvented facts, quotations, and citations in a confident tone. Covered in depth in Module 4.
    SycophancyA tendency to agree with the user. If you suggest an answer, the model may endorse it even when it is wrong.
    Uneven knowledgeStrong on common topics, weaker on local, specialized, recent, or non-English material.
    BiasPatterns in training text, including stereotypes, can appear in examples, names, and images.
    No understanding of your studentsIt does not know your learners, your outcomes, or your context unless you supply them, and it cannot know them the way you do.
    In practice

    STEMA chemistry instructor uses AI to draft ten practice problems. She solves each one herself before posting. Two answer keys are wrong, and one problem has no valid answer. Fixing them takes five minutes and saves a week of student confusion.

    CTEAn HVAC instructor asks for a troubleshooting checklist. The list is sensible but generic and misses the safety lockout step his program requires. He keeps the structure and adds the program-specific steps.

    1.4

    Human-centered use: what stays human

    UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Teachers (2024) starts with a human-centered mindset, ahead of AI foundations and pedagogy. The idea is simple. AI should extend what educators and students can do, and people stay accountable for decisions that affect learners. EDUCAUSE's 2024 AI literacy framework makes a similar point by pairing technical understanding with evaluative skills and ethical considerations.

    A practical way to apply this is to ask three questions about any task before you bring AI into it:

    1. What is the essential human contribution? Judgment about a student, a relationship, disciplinary expertise, or a value choice usually belongs here.
    2. What would go wrong if the output were wrong? This sets the stakes.
    3. Who can inspect and override the result? If no one can, the task is not ready for AI.

    The opportunity-risk map

    Your first portfolio artifact maps your course across seven areas. For each area, note one possible AI use, the main risk, and what must stay under your control.

    While you build your map, use only course materials you would be comfortable posting publicly. Do not paste student work, rosters, grades, or accommodation information into any tool your institution has not approved for that data.

    1.5

    Choosing a pilot problem

    The rest of this course is built around one real teaching problem. A good pilot problem is small, recurring, and low or moderate in stakes. It is also a problem you already care about solving, with or without AI.

    Stronger pilot problemWeaker pilot problem
    "Students struggle to apply the concept to new cases. I need more varied practice cases each week.""I want to use AI in my course."
    "My feedback on lab report drafts arrives too late to help. I want faster formative comments.""I want AI to grade final exams."
    "My readings assume background some students lack. I want a short primer at two reading levels.""I want a chatbot that answers every student question."

    The stronger problems name a learning need and a way to tell if things improved. The weaker ones name a tool, or they hand over a decision that should stay human.

    Write your problem statement with four parts: the problem, who it affects, a success criterion you could observe, and what must remain under your control. Then document your current workflow so you have a baseline to compare against.

    In practice

    Student supportAn academic advisor wants to cut the time spent writing the same orientation emails. Her success criterion is "same accuracy, half the drafting time." What stays human: every message is checked against current deadlines, and any message about a specific student's situation is written by her.

    HumanitiesA history instructor wants more primary-source practice. He decides AI may draft guiding questions, but it may not select or summarize the sources, because source selection is the disciplinary skill he is teaching.

    Judgment check · unscored practice

    Test your judgment

    Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

    Q1.A colleague says, "The chatbot looked up the 2019 study and summarized it for me." The tool has no web search and no attached documents. What is the most accurate description of what happened?

    Question 1 options
    Best answer: A. Without search or documents, the model generates text from learned patterns. The summary might be close, partly wrong, or about a study that does not exist. Only the study itself can confirm it.
    • B. Training stores patterns in model weights, not retrievable copies of documents to condense on demand.
    • C. Older material is what training covers. The issue is whether the output matches the source, not the date.
    • D. A tool without search cannot browse. Tools that do search usually show links you can open.

    Q2.Which task is the best candidate for a first AI pilot in a course?

    Question 2 options
    Best answer: B. It is recurring, low to moderate in stakes, uses no student data, and keeps you in control through a clear check before anything reaches students.
    • A. Final grades are high stakes and must stay under human judgment, even when the rubric is yours.
    • C. Disability information is highly protected, and errors in accommodation letters carry serious consequences.
    • D. Office hours carry the relationship and judgment this module identifies as the essential human contribution.

    Q3.Your LMS releases Module 2 only after a student scores 80% or higher on Quiz 1. Which category is this?

    Question 3 options
    Best answer: C. A fixed if-then rule written by a person controls the release. Nothing is learned from data and nothing new is generated. You check it by checking the rule.
    • A. Nothing is generated.
    • B. The rule does not learn from examples; 80% was set by a person.
    • D. A human set the rule, but the system applies it automatically every time.

    Q4.You run the same prompt twice and get two different lists of discussion questions. What explains this?

    Question 4 options
    Best answer: D. Variation is built in. It helps brainstorming and is a reason to verify facts, because a different run could produce a different claim.
    • A. This is expected behavior, not a fault. Identical output is not the design.
    • B. Models do not retrain on your prompt between requests. The conversation context may change, but the weights do not.
    • C. Providers update models occasionally, not between two requests made minutes apart. The variation happens within the same model.

    Q5.You have two explanations of a concept. One is polished and confident. The other is rough but written by a colleague in the field. How should you decide which to build on?

    Question 5 options
    Best answer: B. Fluency is not evidence and authorship is not proof. Checking claims decides accuracy, and editing fixes style. A rough accurate draft is usually faster to improve than a polished wrong one.
    • A. Polish can hide errors, as the seasons example shows.
    • C. Humans make mistakes too. The rule is to check claims, whoever wrote them.
    • D. Merging can carry the errors of both into one confusing text, and students lack the expertise to sort them.
