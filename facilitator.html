    Course map › Facilitator guide

    For leads and co-facilitators

    Facilitator Guide

    The jobModel the practice you teach: short explanation, visible judgment, real artifacts, peer critique, and honest failure.

    Participants: 12–24Live: 8 × 60 minFieldwork: 8 × ~60 minDirect teaching: ≤ 15 min per session

    The facilitator role

    A learning designer, not a product trainer

    The lead facilitator combines AI literacy, instructional design, assessment knowledge, and group facilitation. The role is to make expert judgment visible, keep every activity tied to a real course, and route institutional problems to the right office instead of treating them as individual faculty deficits.

    Invite short contributions from a librarian (Module 4), an accessibility specialist (Module 5), a privacy or security professional (Modules 2 and 4), and an academic-integrity or faculty-governance representative (Module 7). Ten minutes from the right person is worth more than a slide about their office.

    Responsible use

    Participants should repeatedly see imperfect outputs. Polished demonstrations alone create an unrealistic picture of reliability. Every live demo in this guide includes a failure on purpose.

    Participants and setup

    Who, how many, and with what

    • 12 to 24 participants per facilitator; add table coaches or co-facilitators for larger groups.
    • Cross-disciplinary enrollment with optional discipline breakouts.
    • Institution-approved accounts tested before the first session.
    • Fictional and de-identified practice data only.
    • A versioned facilitator update log, because tools and policies change during the program.

    Institutional prerequisites

    Training cannot replace missing infrastructure

    Confirm each item before launch. If an item is missing, say so to participants and name the interim process.

    PrerequisiteStatus and owner
    One institution-approved general-purpose AI tool with a no-cost or institution-funded participant pathway[tool, license, owner]
    A sandbox that uses fictional, public, or de-identified content only[location]
    A current institutional AI policy, or a clearly labeled interim-policy process[link, date]
    Named contacts: teaching center, library, accessibility office, information security and privacy, academic integrity[names, emails]
    A non-AI alternative for every required activity, so participation never depends on payment, disclosing personal data, or accepting an unapproved service[confirmed by]
    Data classifications and which tools are approved for each class[link]
    Local policy · fill in for your institution

    Paste the same contacts into the Local policy callouts in each module before launch, so participants see local answers in context.

    Course build track

    Sessions teach; fieldwork builds

    The live sessions cover every module in full. The fieldwork produces one teachable unit through the Course Build Studio: a repository of the participant's files (Week 2), a unit framework (Week 3), validated in Week 4, lessons (Week 5), activities (Week 6), an assessed unit (Week 7), and a pilot plan (Week 8). Each module page shows that week's build step at the end of its fieldwork.

    • Before Week 2: confirm the approved tool has a project or file-upload space and that the library can answer license questions about course readings.
    • Watch for participants pasting builder output straight into their course. Ask to see the check-prompt result and the revisions in every gallery and peer critique.
    • Scope: one unit of 3 to 6 lessons. Participants who finish early build a second unit; those who fall behind finish the framework and two lessons, which still meets the pilot-ready standard.
    • Fading: Builders 1 and 2 write the whole prompt; Builder 3 leaves quality criteria to the participant; Builder 4 leaves the five safeguards. Point this out in Week 5 so participants see their growing role.

    Module facilitation notes

    What to emphasize and what to watch for

    ModuleEmphasisWatch forCore live activity
    1 AI fundamentalsLLM fluency is not evidence of truth, understanding, or pedagogical quality.Participants who already use AI daily may want to skip the concepts. Ask them to predict the next-token demo first; most predictions reveal a gap worth ten minutes.Card sort; fluent-versus-accurate comparison; opportunity-risk map.
    2 Tool ecosystemTeach categories and evaluation habits, not a catalog of current products.Brand debates. Redirect every "which tool is best" question to "best for which task, data, and learner?"Tool stations on one shared task; scorecard with hard stops.
    3 Prompt and workflowPrompting is specification plus evaluation, not a collection of magic phrases.Participants rewording prompts randomly. Require a written diagnosis before every revision.Weak-prompt rebuild; paired prompt clinic on an edge case.
    4 ValidationDepth of checking scales with stakes. Lateral reading beats asking the model to check itself.Teams find the numerical error and fake citation but miss the omission. Ask "whose story is missing?" before revealing.TRACE red-team relay on the flawed mini-lesson.
    5 Materials and copyrightImprove, do not merely generate. Copyright questions go to the institution, not to the facilitator's opinion.Raw AI output presented as finished. The artifact must show substantive human revision.Improve lab; peer review for alignment, load, representation, and accessibility.
    6 Student AI literacyStudents should do the thinking the course is meant to develop; AI gets a bounded role.Activities where AI does the target skill. Use the responsibility map to make that visible.Faculty experience an activity as students, then redesign it.
    7 Assessment and policyAlign permission with outcomes. A detector score is never sole evidence. Final grades stay human.Redesigns that only make AI inconvenient. Peers ask: does the task still measure the outcome?Assignment stress test; AIAS leveling; policy draft.
    8 Ethics and implementationEthics is operational: name stakeholders, trade-offs, and a proportionate decision. End with a bounded pilot.Abstract debate. Keep every discussion tied to a case and a documented decision.Ethics case clinic; gallery walk; two-minute pilot proposals.

    Each module page holds the full minute-by-minute run sheet, fieldwork, and judgment check. The run sheets assume the same seven moves every week, so participants spend energy on content rather than logistics.

    Engagement moves

    Eight moves that keep the studio active

    Bring-your-own-course design

    Every lab modifies a real course, assignment, rubric, or instructional item.

    Discipline triads

    Group each participant with one disciplinary peer and one cross-disciplinary peer.

    Case choice

    Offer humanities, STEM, professional or clinical, career-technical, and student-support variants of each case.

    Artifact gallery

    Keep a permission-based repository of prompts, activities, policies, rubrics, and validation examples.

    Failure of the week

    Open each session with one participant example where AI failed and what was learned.

    Student voice

    Include anonymized student concerns or a student panel in Modules 6 to 8 when feasible.

    No-shame differentiation

    Every module has a foundation and an extension path so novices finish the core task and experienced users go further.

    Limit direct presentation to about 10 to 15 minutes per session. Use prediction, comparison, case analysis, production, peer critique, and revision for the rest.

    Discipline variants

    Offer a case for every room

    DisciplineExample cases
    HumanitiesSource-based essay with AI-assisted outlining; validation of quotations against primary texts; a policy for translation tools.
    STEMWorked-solution generation with recomputation; lab-report feedback; code explanation with a test-before-trust rule.
    Health and clinicalDosage and unit checks; de-identified case scenarios; patient-education handouts checked for reading level.
    Career and technicalProcedure checklists checked against manufacturer or code requirements; simulated customer scenarios; safety-critical steps kept human.
    Student supportAdvising FAQ drafts; outreach messages checked for tone and accuracy; strict limits on any student-record use.

    Measure more than satisfaction

    EndPortfolio demonstration, repeated readiness inventory, comparison of original and redesigned artifacts for alignment, validation, transparency, inclusion, and feasibilityEvidence of capability, not just satisfaction
    30 daysDid the pilot launch? What changed?Implementation, not intention

    Treat AI literacy as an iterative professional capability rather than one-time compliance training. The 30/60/90-day review forms are in the resource kit.
