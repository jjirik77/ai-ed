    Before Week 1 · Orientation

    Orientation and Readiness

    Driving questionWhat will you be able to do in eight weeks, and where are you starting from?

    Time: ~30 min before Week 1Interactive: Readiness radarYou bring: One real course

    Overview

    A studio, not a tool tour

    This program is an eight-week faculty learning studio. Each week has one 60-minute live session and about one hour of applied work. That is 8 contact hours and about 16 hours in total. You work on one real course the whole time, and you leave with a ready-to-pilot AI Integration Portfolio.

    The program does not teach you to use a particular product. Products change every few months. Instead, it builds habits that last: a working mental model of how AI systems behave, a way to judge tools, a way to write and test prompts, a way to check output, and a way to decide what must stay human. A well-reasoned decision not to use AI in your course is a valid outcome.

    Before Week 1, read this page, fill in your institution's details in section 0.5, complete the readiness inventory in section 0.6, and choose the course you will work on.

    Keep your progress

    Everything you type saves in this browser as you go. To keep it safe and use it on another computer, select Save progress. You will find that button under every form in the course, and each one saves all of your work to one file. Next time, select Open progress file and choose that file. If you already have a progress file, open it now.

    Predict before you read

    Think of your colleagues. What share of faculty do you expect would call themselves advanced or expert in AI literacy? What share would say their institution has made it clear how AI can be used in teaching?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    In the Digital Education Council's 2025 global faculty survey, 17% placed themselves at an advanced or expert level, and 80% said there was a lack of clarity on how AI can be applied in teaching at their institution. If you feel unsure, you are in the majority, and the gap is partly institutional, not personal.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Describe the weekly rhythm of live studio and fieldwork and how each week feeds the portfolio. Understand
    • Explain the eight capability areas the program develops and the evidence each produces. Understand
    • Rate your current readiness in each capability area and name your two lowest areas. Evaluate
    • Select one course and one teaching problem to carry through the program. Evaluate

    Study the material

    Getting started

    0.1

    Why this program exists

    Faculty and students are already using AI, but use is not the same as literacy. Two global surveys by the Digital Education Council show the gap.

    GroupFinding
    Faculty (2025; 1,681 faculty at 52 institutions in 28 countries)40% feel they are just beginning their AI-literacy journey. Only 17% place themselves at an advanced or expert level. 80% say there is a lack of clarity on how AI can be applied in teaching at their institution. Only 6% fully agree that their institution has provided sufficient resources to build faculty AI literacy.
    Students (2024; 3,839 students in 16 countries)86% regularly use AI in their studies. 58% feel they do not have sufficient AI knowledge and skills, and 48% do not feel prepared for an AI-enabled workplace.

    The pattern matters for design. Students are using AI with or without guidance, so the question in most courses is not whether AI is present. The question is whether anyone is teaching students to use it critically. Faculty, meanwhile, are asked to make course decisions without clear institutional answers. This program gives you a method for making those decisions well, in your discipline, with colleagues.

    In practice

    Student supportAn advising coordinator notices students using AI to draft appeal letters. Some letters are strong; others include invented policy language. The coordinator does not need a product demonstration. The coordinator needs a way to decide what help is appropriate and how students can check the result.

    0.2

    The eight capabilities you will build

    The program is organized around eight capability areas. They draw on the UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers, which organizes teacher competence into a "human-centred mindset", ethics of AI, AI foundations and applications, AI pedagogy, and AI for professional learning, and on the EDUCAUSE AI literacy framework's four areas of technical understanding, evaluative skills, practical application, and ethical considerations.

    CapabilityWhat effective practice looks likeEvidence in your portfolio
    Conceptual literacyExplain AI, machine learning, generative AI, LLMs, training, inference, tokens, context, probabilistic output, and common limitations in plain languageOpportunity-risk map and exit-ticket prediction (Module 1)
    Intentional useDecide whether AI is appropriate for a specific educational problem before selecting a toolAI opportunity-risk map tied to a real course
    Tool judgmentCompare tools on learning alignment, evidence and reliability, privacy and security, accessibility, equity and access, transparency and control, cost and sustainability, and integration and supportCompleted tool-evaluation scorecard
    Prompt and workflow designSpecify goals, context, inputs, constraints, output format, quality criteria, and a review loopTested reusable prompt or workflow with iteration notes
    ValidationVerify factual claims, citations, data, calculations, bias, completeness, and alignment before useAnnotated AI-output audit
    Pedagogical integrationUse AI to support learning outcomes, active learning, feedback, learner variability, and student metacognitionAccessible instructional material (Module 5) and student AI-literacy activity (Module 6)
    Assessment stewardshipRedesign assessments, clarify acceptable AI use, preserve valid evidence of learning, and keep final grading authority humanRevised assignment, rubric, disclosure, and course policy
    Ethical agencyProtect privacy, intellectual property, access, autonomy, and fairness; document when AI is used and when it should not beEthics decision record and 30-day pilot plan
    0.3

    How each week works

    Every live session follows the same structure, so you spend your attention on the work, not on figuring out the format. Direct presentation stays under 15 minutes. The rest is prediction, comparison, hands-on work on your own course, and peer critique.

    MinutesMovePurpose
    0–5Check-inRetrieval prompt, poll, or "what changed in practice?" to activate prior learning and surface barriers
    5–15ConceptOnly the theory needed for the day's decisions, with one contrasting example
    15–25Think-aloudThe facilitator works a live example out loud so expert judgment and checking are visible
    25–43ApplicationYou apply the concept to your own course
    43–53Peer critiqueA short protocol to improve the artifact and learn across disciplines
    53–58CommitmentTurn feedback into one usable next step
    58–60Exit ticketCheck understanding and name support needs

    Between sessions, the roughly 60 minutes of fieldwork follow a 10–40–10 pattern: 10 minutes to review a short resource, 40 minutes to apply the week's method to a real course artifact, and 10 minutes to post your work and give one peer useful feedback.

    You are allowed to conclude "not here"

    This program does not assume that AI use, or training in it, automatically improves productivity or teaching. Every activity therefore solves a real teaching problem and permits a reasoned conclusion not to use AI. That conclusion counts toward completion when it is based on learning alignment, risk, accessibility, workload, or lack of evidence.

    0.4

    Your portfolio and how it is judged

    Instead of eight unrelated homework submissions, you build one portfolio. Each module adds one artifact. By Week 8 the pieces form a plan you can actually run.

    1. Course opportunity-risk map and pilot problem (Module 1)
    2. Tool-evaluation scorecard and decision (Module 2)
    3. Reusable prompt or workflow with iteration log (Module 3)
    4. Annotated validation audit and TRACE checklist (Module 4)
    5. Accessible instructional material with quality-assurance record (Module 5)
    6. Student AI-literacy activity and facilitation guide (Module 6)
    7. Revised assessment, rubric, AI-use directions, and course policy (Module 7)
    8. Ethics decision record and 30-day pilot plan (Module 8)

    The portfolio is judged on six criteria, each at four levels: Not yet ready, Developing, Pilot ready, and Shareable model. Completion means Pilot ready on all six.

    CriterionWhat Pilot ready looks like
    Learning alignmentThe artifact supports a named learning outcome or real faculty task; AI is not included for novelty
    Disciplinary qualityContent is accurate, appropriately complex, current where needed, and reviewed by you
    ValidationClaims, citations, calculations, and assumptions are checked; limits and open questions are visible
    Human agencyHuman and AI responsibilities are explicit; consequential decisions can be reviewed and overridden
    Inclusion and governancePrivacy, accessibility, equity, student choice, copyright, and institutional policy are addressed
    Feasibility and evidenceThe plan names the time, access, support, fallback, and evidence that will decide whether the pilot continues

    Completion rewards readiness, not enthusiasm. You do not earn more credit for using more AI.

    Responsible use

    Your portfolio lives in your browser on this site and is never sent anywhere. Even so, build it with fictional, public, or de-identified material. Do not paste student names, grades, or records into it or into any AI tool you use for the fieldwork.

    0.5

    Your institution and local policies

    Training cannot make up for missing infrastructure. Faculty in this kind of program regularly ask for clear guidance, secure tool access, and vetted information about products. Before Week 1, find out which of these exist at your institution. Gaps are worth raising with the facilitator; they are institutional issues, not personal shortcomings.

    PrerequisiteWhy it matters
    At least one institution-approved general-purpose AI tool with a no-cost or institution-funded path for youNo one should need a personal paid account or to accept an unapproved service to participate
    A practice sandbox using fictional, public, or de-identified content onlyYou can experiment without risking student or institutional data
    A current institutional AI policy, or a clearly labeled interim processYour course policy in Module 7 has to sit inside it
    Named contacts in the teaching center, library, accessibility office, information security or privacy office, and academic integrityMost hard questions in this program end with "ask the right office"
    A non-AI alternative for every required activityParticipation never depends on payment, sharing personal data, or agreeing to an unapproved service

    Fill in your institution's details

    Fill in what you know now; leave the rest blank and add it as you learn it. Your answers appear in the Local policy boxes throughout the course, and you can also edit any of them where they appear. They save in this browser only.

    0.6

    Your readiness inventory

    Rate yourself on each of the eight capabilities. Be honest; nobody else sees this. The point is to know where to spend your effort, and to compare at the end of the program. In Module 8 you will rate yourself again, and the portfolio page will show both pictures side by side.

    Scale: 1 new to this, 2 some exposure, 3 can do it with support, 4 can do it and could help a colleague.

    Interactive · decide & check

    Readiness radar

    Move each slider to rate yourself from 1 to 4. The radar updates as you go. Save when you are done; your ratings stay in this browser only.

    Also choose your course. Pick one you will teach within the next two terms, with at least one low- or moderate-stakes teaching problem you would like to improve. Avoid starting with high-stakes grading; you will get to assessment in Module 7 with better tools.

    In practice

    STEMAn introductory chemistry instructor chooses "students cannot explain why a reaction is balanced, only that it is." That problem is specific, low stakes, and easy to measure.

    CTEA welding instructor chooses "safety-procedure handouts are hard for some students to read." That problem connects directly to Module 5 on accessible materials.
