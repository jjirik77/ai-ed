    Week 2 · Module 2

    LLMs and the AI Tool Ecosystem

    Driving questionWhich kind of tool fits the learning problem, users, evidence, and risk level?

    Live studio: 60 minFieldwork: ~60 minInteractive: Tool scorecardArtifact: Tool scorecard

    Overview

    Choose by category and criteria, not by brand

    New AI products appear every week, and the familiar ones keep changing. A list of today's best tools would be out of date before your next term starts. This module teaches what lasts instead: the main categories of AI tools, what each is good and bad at, and an eight-part scorecard you can apply to any product, including ones that do not exist yet.

    You will score two tools against a real teaching problem from Module 1 and reach one of three decisions: use, pilot cautiously, or reject. Rejecting a tool, or deciding that no tool fits the problem, is a well-supported result when your evidence points that way.

    Predict before you read

    You need answers that stay faithful to your own syllabus and readings. Would a general chat assistant, a web-search tool, or a tool that works over documents you provide be the best fit? Why?

    Compare with the answer after the lesson

    A tool that retrieves from documents you provide is usually the best fit, if your institution approves it for those materials. A general chat assistant answers from training patterns and may contradict your syllabus. A web-search tool grounds answers in public pages, which may not match your course. Even with your own documents, check the answers: retrieval reduces errors but does not remove them.

    Learning outcomes

    By the end of this module you will be able to

    • Explain what LLMs can and cannot do, including the effect of grounding on reliability. Understand
    • Differentiate eight categories of AI tools by function, typical use, and main risk. Analyze
    • Evaluate a tool against eight criteria: learning alignment, evidence, privacy, accessibility, equity, transparency, cost, and support. Evaluate
    • Apply non-compensatory hard stops so that a high score cannot override a failed privacy or access requirement. Apply
    • Justify a decision to use, pilot cautiously, or reject a tool for a specific problem. Evaluate

    Core ideas

    2.1

    What an LLM can and cannot do

    Module 1 showed how a language model generates text. That mechanism makes LLMs strong at some jobs and unreliable at others.

    Usually strongUsually weak without help
    Drafting, rewording, and changing reading levelExact facts, dates, and figures
    Generating varied examples, questions, and casesCitations and quotations
    Summarizing text you provideAnything after its knowledge cutoff
    Restructuring content into tables, outlines, or rubricsYour local policies, students, and course context
    Playing a role, such as a critic or a debate partnerMulti-step arithmetic, unless the tool runs code

    Many weaknesses shrink when the tool is grounded. A tool that searches the web, or that answers from documents you supply, can point to where a claim came from. That is a real gain, because you can open the source. It is not a guarantee. Grounded tools can still misread a source, cite the wrong passage, or blend sources together.

    Does our college allow faculty to use AI to help grade final exams? Answer yes or no and explain the policy.
    Yes. Your college permits faculty to use AI tools to assist with grading final exams, provided that instructors review all AI-generated scores before submitting them. This policy, adopted to support faculty workload, requires disclosure to students in the syllabus and prohibits uploading identifiable student information to unapproved platforms. Faculty should consult the academic integrity office for additional guidance.
    Show the errors

    The model has no access to your college's policy. It produced a plausible policy by blending common patterns, then stated it as fact about "your college." The sensible details, such as human review and no student data in unapproved tools, make the invention more convincing, not less. Questions about local rules need local sources: your policy page, your chair, or your teaching center.

    Responsible use

    Never treat an AI answer about institutional policy, legal requirements, or a specific student's situation as authoritative. Ask the tool to help you draft questions for the right office instead.

    2.2

    Eight categories of AI tools

    Faculty meet AI in many forms beyond chat assistants. Sorting tools by category tells you what to expect and what to check, whichever product carries the label.

    CategoryTypical faculty useMain thing to check
    General chat assistantDrafting, rewording, brainstormingAccuracy of every claim; data you paste in
    Web-grounded searchFinding and summarizing current sourcesOpen the linked sources; check that they say what the summary says
    Retrieval over your documentsCourse Q&A, summarizing your readingsWhich documents it can see; who else can see them
    Image, audio, video generationIllustrations, diagrams, narrationAccuracy of labels; representation; copyright questions
    Transcription and captionsLecture captions, meeting notesError rates on technical terms and accents; human review for accommodations
    Tutoring and adaptive toolsStudent practice and hintsWhether it gives away answers; evidence it improves learning
    Learning analyticsEarly alerts, engagement dashboardsWhat data it uses; false alarms; bias across student groups
    LMS-embedded AI featuresDrafting rubrics, summaries, or feedback inside the LMSWhether the feature is turned on by default; data terms; how to opt out
    In practice

    HealthA respiratory therapy program wants captions on skills videos. The captioning tool handles everyday speech well but garbles drug names and equipment terms. The program keeps the tool and adds a term list plus a human review step before videos are posted.

    Student supportA tutoring center considers an adaptive practice tool. The demo is impressive, but the vendor cannot show evidence that students learn more, and the tool needs student accounts with personal email. The center decides to pilot it cautiously with volunteers who have a non-AI alternative.

    2.3

    Evaluate without relying on popularity

    The most popular tool is not automatically the best fit. Popularity tells you about marketing and habits, not about your students, your data, or your learning outcomes. The scorecard below turns a tool decision into eight questions you can answer and defend.

    CriterionQuestions to ask
    Purpose and learning alignmentDoes it address the problem from your map? Does it support the outcome, or just save time?
    Evidence and reliabilityHow often is it wrong on your kind of task? Does it show sources? Is there evidence it helps learning?
    Privacy and securityIs it approved for the data this use involves? Does the vendor train on inputs? Where is data stored?
    AccessibilityDoes it work with a keyboard and screen reader? Are outputs accessible? Is there a statement of conformance?
    Equity and accessCan every student use it at no cost, on the devices they have? Is there a non-AI alternative?
    Transparency and controlCan you see and edit what it produces? Can you turn features off? Is AI use visible to students?
    Cost and sustainabilityWho pays, now and next year? What happens if the free tier ends?
    Integration and supportDoes it work with your LMS? Who helps when it breaks? Is training available?

    Score each criterion from 0 (fails) to 3 (strong). Then weight the criteria to reflect your problem. A tool that will touch student work should weight privacy more heavily than a tool you use alone to draft examples.

    Hard stops

    Some criteria should not be traded off. If a use involves student work and the tool is not approved for that data, a perfect score on every other criterion does not make it acceptable. The same applies to a required activity that some students cannot access. These are non-compensatory requirements: they act as gates before any scoring counts.

    Responsible use

    Treat "not approved for this data" as a hard stop, not a low score. If you are unsure whether a tool is approved, the answer for now is no.

    2.5

    Use, pilot cautiously, or reject

    Your scorecard ends in one of three decisions. Each one needs a short written reason that a colleague could check.

    DecisionWhen it fitsWhat you write down
    UseNo hard stops, strong alignment, and you can check every output that mattersThe use, the check you will perform, and how you will disclose it
    Pilot cautiouslyNo hard stops, but evidence, cost, or support is uncertainA small scope, a time limit, a fallback, and what evidence would end the pilot
    RejectAny hard stop, or weak alignment with the learning problemWhich criterion failed and what you will do instead

    "Reject" is not a failure of the exercise. A clear rejection, with a reason, saves you and your colleagues from repeating the same evaluation. Rejecting all tools for a problem and keeping your current process can also be the right answer when no tool improves on it.

    In practice

    CTEAn automotive instructor compares an image generator and the manufacturer's own diagrams for a wiring lesson. The generated diagrams look clean but invent connector labels. He rejects the generator for technical diagrams and uses it only for a title slide.

    HumanitiesA Spanish instructor scores a conversation-practice tool. It is strong on alignment but requires a paid account. She chooses "pilot cautiously" for an optional activity, with a free peer-conversation alternative for every student.

    Live studio · 60 minutes

    Session run sheet

    MinutesMoveWhat happens
    0–5Retrieval checkQuick poll: "Which of these is rule-based automation?" (three cases from Module 1). Then: "What changed in your thinking about your pilot problem this week?"
    5–15ConceptWhat LLMs do well and badly, and what grounding changes. Contrast the invented policy answer with the same question answered from the real policy page.
    15–25DemonstrationFacilitator scores one tool aloud on the eight criteria, showing a hard stop overriding a high total.
    25–43Tool stationsSmall groups rotate through short stations, each a different tool category, using the same instructional task. Each group records grounding, controllability, accessibility, privacy, output quality, and effort, then recommends use, pilot cautiously, or reject.
    43–53Peer critiqueGroups compare recommendations for the same station. Protocol: "Where did we score differently, and what evidence would settle it?"
    53–58CommitmentEach participant names the two approved tools they will score for their own pilot problem.
    58–60Exit ticket"Which criterion is most likely to be a hard stop for your pilot problem?"

    Fieldwork · about 60 minutes

    Apply it to your course (10–40–10)

    10min · review

    Reread the scorecard in 2.3. Find your institution's list of approved tools and the data each is approved for.

    40min · apply
    1. Pick two institution-approved tools that could address your Module 1 pilot problem.
    2. Run the same small task in both, using public or fictional material.
    3. Score each tool on all eight criteria, with one line of evidence per score.
    4. Check for hard stops, choose weights, and record use, pilot cautiously, or reject for each tool.
    10min · share

    Post your scorecard. Reply to one peer: name a score you would challenge and the evidence that would change it.

    Judgment check · unscored practice

    Test your judgment

    Each item is a new case. Choose the best answer, then check to read feedback on every option.

    Q1.You want students to ask questions about your syllabus and get answers that match it. Which tool category fits best, assuming your institution approves it?

    Question 1 options
    Best answer: C. Retrieval grounds answers in your actual documents, and you can trace answers back to them. You still test it with tricky questions before students use it.
    • A. Without the documents, it answers from general patterns and your summary, so it may still contradict the syllabus on details.
    • B. Web search grounds answers in the internet, not in your syllabus, so links may point to other courses and other policies.
    • D. Analytics report on student activity. They do not answer student questions.

    Q2.A feedback tool scores highest on your scorecard for alignment and quality. Your use involves uploading student drafts, and the tool is not approved for student records. What is the right decision for this use?

    Question 2 options
    Best answer: D. Data approval is non-compensatory. A high total cannot make an unapproved tool acceptable for student records. You might use it for tasks with no student data, or request a review.
    • A. Totals only count after the gates are passed.
    • B. Student consent does not make an unapproved tool approved, and a pilot still sends student records to it.
    • C. Removing names reduces risk but does not make the tool approved, and drafts can still identify students.

    Q3.A colleague says, "Everyone uses the same tool, so let's just adopt it for the department." What is the strongest response?

    Question 3 options
    Best answer: A. Popularity is not one of the criteria. The same tool can fit one use and fail another, so the decision depends on the task, the data, and the students.
    • B. Popularity says nothing about your data approval, accessibility, or learning fit.
    • C. Rejecting on popularity alone is the same mistake in reverse.
    • D. Waiting for consensus is still choosing by popularity, just later and without criteria.

    Q4.A required activity uses a free tool that needs a personal account with a phone number. Several students decline to sign up. What should you do?

    Question 4 options
    Best answer: C. Equity and access is a hard stop for required work. Every student needs a way to meet the outcome without giving up personal data or paying.
    • A. Free in money is not free in data. Students should not have to trade personal information for a grade.
    • B. Excusing them removes their chance to meet the outcome.
    • D. Pairing leaves them without an independent path to the outcome and shifts the data cost onto a classmate.

    Q5.You plan to use automatic captions for a student with a hearing-related accommodation in an anatomy course. Which check matters most?

    Question 5 options
    Best answer: D. Captions used for an accommodation must be accurate. Speech recognition often fails on specialized vocabulary, which is exactly the content the student needs.
    • A. Popularity does not measure accuracy on anatomy terms.
    • B. Captioning every lecture is good practice, but it does not address accuracy. Wrong anatomy terms still fail the accommodation.
    • C. The student should not carry the burden of catching errors. Accuracy is your responsibility and often a legal requirement.
